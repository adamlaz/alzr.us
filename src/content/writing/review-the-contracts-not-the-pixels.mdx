I run engineering at a consumer product that helps families preserve their memories. Boxes of VHS, VHS-C, 8mm, and MiniDV tapes arrive at the plant, get cleaned and digitized on capture rigs, and come out as video files. Before a box ships back, an inspector at a keyboard-first kiosk scans the box barcode, jumps across each digitized video, and records `Pass`, `Fail` (with symptoms), `Pass with exception`, or `Could not inspect`.

We built that station in four days. Right before it launched, an unexpected family emergency pulled me out of the office. I was told I didn't need to work at all -- and for the screen, I didn't. Over the first month, our product manager shipped 19 pull requests to that repo, self-merging 14 on green CI in a median of six minutes, while I dropped in on the contracts when I wanted a distraction.

## Ten days on the seams, four days to the floor

Our product manager wrote the requirements doc ten days before anyone touched the UI. We spent those ten days deciding which service owned each piece of state: where a box's tape list comes from, where digitized files live, where verdicts are saved so stations share history, and what triggers a recapture.

Once those boundaries were settled, I wrote the build plan and an "Agent Build Brief" on Sunday night. Monday morning, one of our engineers handed it to a coding agent, and the v1 front-end pull request opened before 10 AM -- 41 files running end to end on local fixtures.

Monday and Tuesday I added CI gates (format, unit tests, build, and browser tests) and wired the real adapters and back-end endpoints. Wednesday morning, a family emergency put me on a plane. The station still went live on Thursday without me in the building and entered daily floor use on Monday.

## What went into the brief

If you hand a coding agent a product doc with open UI choices and staging dependencies, it guesses at the boundaries and writes two thousand lines of code it can't run.

The Sunday-night brief opened with two lines:

> Who this is for- [our engineer], and the coding agent [he] hands it to. Every decision in here is already made. If something reads as a choice, treat it as a bug in this document and ask rather than picking.
>
> Target- something reviewable tomorrow.

Then it set six rules:

1. **Standalone.** Server-rendered web app, no shared state with other front ends.
2. **Every external system behind an adapter with a mock.** Order lookup, playable media links, saved inspection records, and floor notifications.
3. **Keep order state and media state in their own services.** The order service lists which tapes belong to a box; the media and inspection services own captured files and grading history.
4. **Runs completely on mocks with no network.** Local sample orders and bundled test videos run the station: *"That is the definition of not-blocked, and it is also how the agent verifies its own work."*
5. **No database, no auth.** Locked floor network; the inspection API is the durable record.
6. **Do not build a design system, do not pull in a component library.** On day one, we didn't know which controls would survive real boxes.

The brief ended with acceptance checks the agent had to run itself in mock mode -- scanning sample order `1001`, grading both tapes, and checking that a failed tape blocks the order-passed notification.

Building v1 also caught a bug in my brief. I had used the playback URL as the file's ID in the saved record. While wiring the media adapter, the engineer and the agent noticed that production issues expiring signed URLs. Reopening an order an hour later -- or comparing a recapture against Round 1 -- would see a different string for the same file. They split the contract into a permanent storage reference and a temporary playback link.

## Reviewing the app before writing a line of code

When the v1 pull request opened Monday morning, the PM didn't need a staging deploy. Because it ran on mocks, he reviewed the flow in pull-request comments against what an inspector actually knows at the desk:

- *"There's a field for rack number, but how could I know that at this stage?"*
- *"I'm not reviewing the tapes, just the files. QA will diagnose whether it's the tape or the capture."* (cutting my recapture checkbox).
- Autoplay between tapes so an eleven-tape box didn't take eleven `Space` presses.
- Keeping `Unsure` (later `Could not inspect`): *"I'm fine leaving it for now and seeing if I find a good use for it in the first couple weeks."*

We cut rack number and the recapture checkbox, added autoplay, and merged v1. Thursday it was on the floor.

## Floor loops in a single afternoon

Once the station was in daily use on Monday, the PM started shipping code with Claude Code. His first pull request landed that day: a read-only QA review page where a lead could inspect failed orders, symptoms, and notes against the mock reader. I approved it with two commits on top.

Two days later, he sat with inspectors and filed eight issues from real boxes: progress counters, focus after saving a note, digitizer notes from the capture rig, symptom labels, and submit guards. One needed a new field from the order service; the other seven closed in a single pull request four and a half hours later, merged eight minutes after opening:

> Merging this myself since it's just UI changes on the station.

The next day, while I was still away with family, we shipped recapture rounds and `"Passed with exception"`. When a failed tape comes back from a second capture pass, the station shows Round 1's failure, plays the new file, and lets the inspector pass it or record an exception. The PM built the UI flow. When I wanted an hour to think about something else that night, I opened my laptop, walked his branch through three capture rounds in mock mode, pushed 12 commits on top, and shipped the back-end change alongside it.

## Green CI for the screen, human review for the contracts

If the PM had to wait on me for every button label or shortcut change, floor fixes would take days. If an agent-written UI tweak changed an API payload, it could break real boxes at the desk. So we split the repo in the first line of the code-owners file:

> Reviewers requested automatically. No approval is required to merge; green CI is.

Green CI is required on `main`; human approval isn't. I'm a code owner only on the adapters and CI config. Anything on the screen self-merges on green checks, and every pull request has a four-box `"Station impact"` checklist: no change to the live station, needs a station update, changes what goes to an external service, or needs an upstream change first.

Mocks can still drift from production. When a pull request touches a contract, I check out the branch and push commits directly instead of leaving comments.

In week four, the PM added multi-station sync so stations read each other's saved results from the inspection API. Walking his branch, I found the mock accepting state transitions that production rejects. I pushed one commit with six fixes:

> The mock now refuses what [the back end] refuses... A couple of our tests were passing on states prod can't reach.

<PullQuote>
  If the mock accepts a payload that production rejects, the test suite is lying to the person merging the pull request.
</PullQuote>

Two days later, he opened a large QA-review pull request. Looking at the diff, I wrote: *"Most of what needs to change is in the three contracts, not the station."* That one stayed open while we sorted out the contracts underneath it.

## Out of the office on both sides

When the family emergency hit, I was told I didn't need to work at all. For the station's UI, I really didn't -- the PM was shipping floor fixes in minutes without me.

Honestly, I needed the distraction, and building like this is fun. A couple of years ago, checking in for an hour from out of town meant answering messages or sitting in a meeting. You couldn't actually build something in the margins of a hard week and then shut your laptop. With coding agents and a repo that runs on local mocks, I could open my laptop when I wanted a break, wire a contract or tighten a test suite in an hour, and close it again without holding anyone up.

Back-end work ran the same way. Right after building the QA review page on mocks, the PM filed an issue with the exact read endpoint his page needed. The issue was the contract. When I had a quiet window, I picked it up with an agent, shipped the endpoint the next day, and shipped watched-ranges the day after.

Across weeks two and three, our schedules barely overlapped. I was in and out with family the first two weeks, and the PM left on a planned two-week break right after. On the first Sunday of his break, still out of town and looking for a few hours of focus, I hardened the repo across six pull requests:

1. **Code owners and the pull-request template**, setting up green-CI self-merge and the `"Station impact"` checklist.
2. **Per-test order data**, so parallel browser tests stopped fighting over sample order `1001`.
3. **DOM video events instead of sleeps**, waiting on real playback and seek events rather than fixed timers.
4. **Keyboard shortcuts defined in one file**, so the UI and the key listener can't drift apart.
5. **A split README**, with separate sections for floor ops, product, and back-end engineers.

When the PM got back in week four, he merged 11 pull requests in eight days without a catch-up meeting -- digitizer notes, recapture slip printing with our engineer, an interactive training lab, and floor polish.

In the first month, all 46 of the PM's commits carried a Claude co-author trailer. He self-merged 14 of his 19 pull requests in a median of six minutes; I merged 15, approved 3 of his, and pushed 19 commits onto 6 of his branches.

## "It looks very much like a Claude artifact"

At the end of week four, the PM shared the new training lab (`"the tool teaches the job"`). Our chief product officer looked at the screens and said the UI `"looks very much like a Claude artifact"`: too much text, few glyphs or big buttons, lots of tracked all-caps eyebrow labels, and too dense. His target was `"highly visual, low cognitive overhead, more like kiosks for checking in at the airport or ordering a Jamba juice"`, backed by written rules and automated benchmarks.

The PM pushed back on one point: the station runs on keyboard muscle memory at floor speed, so anyone redesigning it needed to take the training first and keep the keys, flows, and saves untouched.

My day-one rule -- *"Do not build a design system, do not pull in a component library"* -- got v1 live in four days, and five weeks later its absence was why the screen looked like an AI artifact. Every pull request had invented another bordered card, caption, and tracked uppercase header. Auditing the old tape screen turned up **148 words**, **34 controls**, **26 keycaps**, every shortcut printed twice, and **more than 25 tracked all-caps labels**.

That Sunday -- one day, 29 commits, 212 files -- I reversed the day-one rule. We built a 14-piece station kit adapted from our app's design system (shadcn-based, with React-free variant recipes so server-rendered pages never load React) and a component gallery showing every piece in every state. We moved the station, QA review, and training onto the kit, deleted the legacy CSS, and enforced four rules in browser tests:

- **At most 15 UI words per state, locked to the exact count.** A browser helper counts visible words and fails the build if a state goes over budget.<Note id="1" /> On the tape screen, the back arrow and `Esc` lost their text labels, the note box folded behind `Note (N)`, and the duplicate shortcut footer was deleted.
- **Video takes at least 60% of the screen, with nothing smaller than 20 px.** Small text slows people down at arm's length.<Note id="2" /> Browser tests check the video area and call `getComputedStyle()` to ban type under 20 px and tracked all-caps labels.
- **`Pass` and `Fail` get equal weight, with one keycap per control.** When most tapes pass, a huge green `Pass` button trains people to miss defects.<Note id="3" /> All four verdicts sit in an even 2x2 grid with custom icons. Key presses flash for `120 ms` and saves play a short tone per outcome.
- **No `"Are you sure?"` dialogs, and barcode scans can't trigger shortcuts.** Confirm dialogs just teach people to double-tap `Enter`, so saves happen immediately and `Z` undoes the last tape. Barcode scanners fire digits every `40 ms` followed by `Enter`; a timing guard catches scanner bursts before they hit symptoms `1` through `8`.

Once we finished trimming copy across all nine states (giving Round 2 a few extra words to show what failed in Round 1), we locked each state's ceiling in CI to its exact count. Adding one word to the `8`-word tape screen or `3`-word fail picker breaks the build.

Training moved onto the actual station screen too, replacing its old three-column layout. A one-line coach strip sits next to the transport bar, shows the next key and progress pips, and pulses a ring on the live button. When a trainee catches a defect, it jumps to the fault timestamp and explains what they saw (`"Right: torn lines from your first D press"`). CI caps the coach strip at 12 words on a single line.

## Owning the seams

<GuardrailStackDiagram />

I don't want to be in the approval path for button labels, shortcut legends, or symptom copy -- whether I'm at my desk or out of town with family. I want to own the seams where a bad merge can corrupt production data, and make everything else safe to self-merge on green CI. That's why the station kept moving when I didn't need to work, and why dropping in for an hour when I wanted a distraction was actually fun. Inside those seams, our product manager owns the station -- down to a line he merged onto the home screen in week four, right above the barcode input:

> Every tape here is a family's memory. Your job is to make sure we send them the best quality possible.
