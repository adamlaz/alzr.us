A friend's name needed to land in the chorus of an EDM festival anthem. You
know the format: a Dutch big-room track built around an F1 driver's name,
shouted over the drop. The job was to keep the record exactly as it is and
change one word, the name, to my friend's. Pure AI. No recording myself, no
hiring a singer.

<Depth max={1}>

It's June 2026. Music models write whole songs from a prompt and voice
cloning is a commodity, so this looked like one evening's work. It wasn't.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

It's June 2026. Music models write entire songs from a text prompt. Voice
cloning is a commodity. Swapping one name in an existing chorus is a strictly
smaller problem than the things these tools demo every week. So this is a
one-evening project. It wasn't, and the gap between "the tools can almost do
this" and "the tools can do this" is the whole story of working at the
bleeding edge right now.

## The task is smaller than it sounds, and harder

Take an existing recording. Keep the melody, the instrumental, the mix, the
vibe. Replace one proper noun in a shouted, vocoded, crowd-chant vocal. That
last clause is the trap. Most of what shipped in 2026 generates new songs from
scratch. I didn't want a new song. I wanted this song, minus one name, plus
another.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

For the record, this was a one-off for one friend, not a track I'd release.
The clips below are short excerpts, here so you can hear the failures instead
of taking my word for them. The one name you can clearly make out, in the
spliced take, is a stand-in, not my friend's. Cutting a full release over a
commercial master is a rights problem I get to skip, because the release was
never the point. The technical problem is the one I cared about.

</Depth>

## The June 2026 landscape, fast

The market splits cleanly on this one task.<Note id="1" /> Most of the 2026
headline models are the wrong tool: they write a whole new track, and I already
have a track.

<Depth max={2}>

One open-source model is built for exactly this job: YingMusic takes a clip
of the melody plus the edited lyrics and sings the line again.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

| Tool | What it is | Does this task? |
|---|---|---|
| LeVo, SongGeneration, ACE-Step | Open-weights song generators | No. They write new songs from a prompt. Brilliant, irrelevant. |
| Suno v5.5 | Commercial covers + section-replace | Only on tracks Suno itself generated, not an arbitrary commercial master. |
| Lyria 3 Pro (Vertex) | Commercial prompt-to-song | Generates, but over-refuses proper nouns in vocals. More below. |
| YingMusic | Open-source diffusion melody-editor | The one built for exactly this: a melody clip plus edited lyrics, no manual alignment. |

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

YingMusic came out of an academic lab and a games company in March 2026, around
seven hundred million parameters, clean 44.1 kHz stereo, no syllable-aligning by
hand. On paper it is the tool. Six months ago it did not exist.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

My notes from before the first run already had the risk written down as an
open question: how would it handle pitched, processed EDM vocals, when what
it trained on was cleaner solo singing? The repo's README adds a constraint
of its own. Everything the model hears has to be a bare vocal, with no
accompaniment, so a finished record has to be pulled apart before the model
can touch it.

</Depth>

## The part the demo videos skip

I cloned it onto an M4 Max and started paying the tax nobody films. The model
ships CUDA-first; getting it onto Apple's GPU was four small patches, none of
them hard:

```python
# 1. route the model to Apple's GPU (the branch the repo didn't have)
def get_device():
    if torch.cuda.is_available():
        return "cuda:0"
    if torch.backends.mps.is_available():
        return "mps"
    return "cpu"

# 2. a CUDA-only quantization dep that has no business on a Mac
#    requirements.txt: bitsandbytes==0.48.1; sys_platform != "darwin"

# 3. pin the audio decoder to the build that matches PyTorch 2.6's ABI
#    requirements.txt: torchcodec==0.10.0

# 4. point phonemizer at the espeak dylib it can't find on macOS
os.environ.setdefault(
    "PHONEMIZER_ESPEAK_LIBRARY",
    "/opt/homebrew/.../libespeak-ng.dylib",
)
```

That's the part the demo videos skip. Forty-five minutes of yak-shaving stood
between "git clone" and the first sound, and in my experience that's the rule
at the bleeding edge.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The first two patches are still sitting in the checkout as an uncommitted
diff. The espeak one never went into the repo. It's an environment default at
the top of my own run script, pointing at Homebrew's espeak-ng 1.52.0. The
environment is Python 3.10 with PyTorch 2.6.0, and the first run downloads
about 2.7 GB of model weights plus a 913 MB checkpoint for the vocal
separator the repo bundles, a Mel-band RoFormer. The clone is stamped 8:04 pm
and the first pair of separated stems 8:36.

espeak is in that list because the model never sees letters. English lyrics
go through espeak into IPA phonemes, the same alphabet the model uses for
Chinese, and the model is conditioned on those. Whatever I typed for the
name, the model got the phonemes espeak chose for it.

</Depth>

## The failure that actually matters

YingMusic ran. It produced clean audio: the melody intact, the timbre
convincing, the mix sitting right. And it could not say the name. The word
came out as a mushy approximation, the consonants smeared, the syllables
merged into a vowel-shaped blur.

<Depth min={3}>

My read of the failure, and it's a read, not a proof: YingMusic was trained on
clean, sustained singing, and a festival chant is shouted, heavily processed,
half-sung, well outside that. Maybe the model generalizes badly past its
distribution. Maybe it's the phoneme conditioning, or the decoder choking on a
vocal style it never saw. I swept the knobs but I didn't isolate the cause, so
I won't pretend I know which it is. The failure was identical across all
fourteen runs: the melody and timbre came out right and the consonants came
out wrong.

### What the fourteen variants changed

Everything ran on a 30-second cut of the first chorus. The cut was separated
once into a vocal stem and an instrumental stem, and both were cached. Every
run then used that one vocal stem twice, as the voice to imitate and as the
melody to follow, and its output was mixed back over the cached instrumental.

The lyrics go in as two lines of phrases separated by pipes: the words on the
recording, and the words I wanted. Mine were eight phrases each, the hook
syllables alternating with the name, and the only difference between the two
lines was the name.

The baseline and the first three variants survive only as filenames: the
defaults, which are guidance 3 and 32 steps, then guidance 5, guidance 5 with
a phonetic spelling, and guidance 7. The other eleven are still in the script
I kept, with their settings.

| What changed | Guidance | Steps | Seed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second word of the name swapped for a real English word that sounds close | 5 | 64 | 7 |
| Name respelled with the stressed syllable in capitals | 5 | 64 | 7 |
| Name respelled the way an English speaker would sound it out | 5 | 64 | 13 |
| Name unchanged, new vocal 1.5 times louder in the mix | 5 | 64 | 7 |
| Name unchanged, new vocal 1.2 times louder | 9 | 96 | 99 |
| A plain four-syllable name, the same count as the original | 5, then 7 | 64 | 7, then 11 |
| A plain five-syllable name | 5, then 7 | 64 | 7, then 11 |
| A plain three-syllable name | 5, then 7 | 64 | 7, then 11 |

The louder-vocal run was aimed at a different suspect. If the smear was the
original shout leaking through the instrumental stem, a new vocal half again
as loud should have covered it. It came out as mushy as the rest.

The table also shows how loose the sweep was. The guidance-9 clip above moved
four things at once (guidance, steps, seed and mix level), and every
guidance-7 name run changed its seed along with the guidance. Three inputs
never moved in any of the eleven: the 30-second cut, the separated stem, and
the line of reference lyrics. So when I say I didn't isolate the cause, this
is how far short of it I stopped.

</Depth>

The call each variant made, from the script, with the lyric strings
shortened and the names taken out:

```python
REF_TEXT = "<hook>|<original name>|<hook>|<original name>|..."  # 8 phrases

def run_variant(name, target_text, cfg, nfe, seed, vocal_gain=1.0):
    final_out = f'{OUT_DIR}/chorus1_{name}.wav'
    if os.path.exists(final_out):          # re-runs skip finished variants
        return final_out

    model = get_model()                    # lazy singleton, loaded once
    audio_tensor, sr = model(
        ref_audio_path=VOCAL_STEM,         # timbre: the separated vocal
        melody_audio_path=VOCAL_STEM,      # melody: the same file
        ref_text=REF_TEXT.strip(),
        target_text=target_text.strip(),
        lrc_align_mode='sentence_level',
        sil_len_to_end=0.5,
        t_shift=0.5,
        nfe_step=nfe,
        cfg_strength=cfg,
        seed=seed,
    )

    tmp = tempfile.mkdtemp()
    vocal_out = os.path.join(tmp, 'v.wav')
    torchaudio.save(vocal_out, audio_tensor.to('cpu'), sample_rate=sr)
    mix(vocal_out, INST_STEM, final_out, vocal_gain=vocal_gain)
    return final_out

VARIANTS = [
    # name, target_text, cfg, nfe, seed, vocal_gain
    ('v5_<real word>',  '<hook>|<first name> <real word>|...',  5.0, 64, 7,  1.0),
    ('v6_stress',       '<hook>|<name, stress in caps>|...',    5.0, 64, 7,  1.0),
    ('v7_anglicized',   '<hook>|<name, anglicized>|...',        5.0, 64, 13, 1.0),
    ('v8_gain15',       '<hook>|<name>|...',                    5.0, 64, 7,  1.5),
    ('v9_cfg9',         '<hook>|<name>|...',                    9.0, 96, 99, 1.2),
    ('<4 syllables>_cfg5', '<hook>|<plain name>|...',           5.0, 64, 7,  1.0),
    ('<4 syllables>_cfg7', '<hook>|<plain name>|...',           7.0, 64, 11, 1.0),
    # ...the same pair again for a 5-syllable and a 3-syllable name
```

`mix()` adds the new vocal times `vocal_gain` to the instrumental and divides
by the peak if the sum goes over 1.0. A failed variant prints its error and
the loop moves on to the next one.

## The pivot, and why it sounded wrong

<Depth max={1}>

If the singing model won't say the word, build the word and paste it in. I
had a cloud text-to-speech voice say the name and a script drop it over each
shout. You could hear the name clearly, and it sounded like a podcast guest
reading it over a house track.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

If the singing model won't say the word, build the word and paste it in. So I
pivoted to a splice pipeline. Separate the vocal stem with Demucs, find every
chant in it with librosa's onset detection, and generate the name with Google's
Chirp 3 HD, the cleanest neutral cloud voice I had. Time-stretch each clip to
fit its slot, pitch it up a few semitones, run it through a bandpass and a
soft-clipper for some festival grit, and drop it into the gaps.

<SpliceDiagram />

It worked, in the sense that you could hear the name clearly. It failed in the
sense that it sounded like a podcast guest reading the name over a house track.
The voice wasn't the weak link; Chirp 3 HD is about as good as neutral TTS
gets. Neutral was the weak link. Read-speech prosody has the wrong texture for
a shouted crowd vocal, and no amount of EQ, distortion, or pitch-shifting moved
it from narrator to drop. Clear words, wrong instrument.

</Depth>

### Where the splice windows landed

The prosody was the failure I could hear. The script I kept shows a second
problem. Its detector takes every onset librosa reports on the vocal stem, keeps
one only if it comes at least 3 seconds after the last one kept, and drops
any that land in near-silence. Re-run on the cached stem, that is 52 raw
onsets cut to six. The first, at 9.10 seconds, was a background sample, and I
removed it with a hard-coded "ignore anything before 14 seconds". Each of the
five left got a window 2 seconds long.

<AiMusicChantWindows />

The vocal in that chorus repeats every 3.75 seconds, which is two bars at 128
beats per minute. My five windows start 3.0 to 3.3 seconds apart, because
with 52 onsets to choose from there is always one just past the 3-second
gap. So each window begins roughly half a second to three quarters of a
second earlier in the loop than the one before it, and the pasted name sits at a
different place in the bar every time. The spliced clip above differs from
the source in those same five places.

Inside a window the original vocal is faded out over 60 milliseconds and the
new clip faded in; outside the windows the original stem plays untouched.
Wherever a window missed part of a shout, the old name is still in the mix.
I rendered a second version that kept 30% of the original under the new
voice as a texture bed.

Two steps from the description above ran differently in this script. It
never calls a separator. It reads the two stem files the first script had
already cached, and that script used the separator bundled in the model's
repo. And the time-stretch only fires when a clip is longer than its window.
I generated the name in five voices and took two into the splice; the five
clips run 1.48 to 1.92 seconds, all under the 2-second window, so each was
centered in its window with silence on both sides and never stretched.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The detector and the processing chain from the script, trimmed, with the
drop counts from the re-run added as comments:

```python
def detect_onsets(audio_path, min_gap_sec=3.0):
    y, sr = librosa.load(audio_path, sr=44100, mono=True)
    onset_frames = librosa.onset.onset_detect(
        y=y, sr=sr, hop_length=512, backtrack=True, units='time', delta=0.05,
    )
    rms = librosa.feature.rms(y=y, hop_length=512)[0]
    times = librosa.frames_to_time(np.arange(len(rms)), sr=sr, hop_length=512)
    def rms_at(t):
        return float(rms[np.argmin(np.abs(times - t))])
    onsets = []
    for t in onset_frames:
        t = float(t)
        if onsets and t - onsets[-1] < min_gap_sec:
            continue                      # dropped 44 of 52
        if rms_at(t) < 0.02:
            continue                      # dropped 2 more
        onsets.append(t)
    return onsets, len(y) / sr

# window = (onset, min(onset + 2.0, next onset))
chant_windows = [w for w in windows if w[0] > 14.0]   # the hand cut

def process_tts_edm(audio, sr=44100, pitch_steps=3, drive=2.5):
    audio = librosa.effects.pitch_shift(audio.astype(np.float32), sr=sr, n_steps=pitch_steps)
    sos = butter(4, [180, 6000], btype='bandpass', fs=sr, output='sos')
    audio = sosfilt(sos, audio).astype(np.float32)
    audio = np.tanh(audio * drive) / np.tanh(drive)   # soft clip
    return np.clip(audio, -0.95, 0.95).astype(np.float32)
```

Window starts from the re-run: 15.13, 18.44, 21.46, 24.75 and 27.77 seconds.
The 3.75-second figure is the autocorrelation peak of the stem's RMS envelope
from 14 seconds on. Stepping by that figure from the first onset would have
kept all five windows on the grid.

</Depth>

## The refusal

<Depth max={1}>

For completeness I asked Google's Lyria 3 Pro to generate the whole track
from a described prompt. It rejected every version that had a name in the
lyrics. I removed the name itself, and it generated happily.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

For completeness I tried Lyria 3 Pro, generating the whole thing from a
described prompt. The content filter rejected it: sensitive words. I stripped
the references to real artists and festivals. Still rejected. I stripped
everything down to a generic genre description and the bare name. Rejected. I
removed the name itself, and it generated happily.

</Depth>

<Callout variant="note" title="Where the gate actually sits">
  Lyria's filter wasn't objecting to a real person. It blocks proper nouns in
  sung lyrics as a class.<Note id="2" /> A name in a vocal line trips it whether the name is
  a celebrity or your buddy. The cloud tool over-refuses by category, which is
  a defensible safety choice and a useless one for the thing I was doing.
</Callout>

<Depth min={4}>

### What the Lyria prompts said

The first prompt was long. It opened with style anchors (genre, tempo, key,
the kick pattern, the vocal treatment) and named the three artists behind the
original. Then came nine timestamped sections from 0:00 to 2:50, each with
its arrangement and its lyric lines, the name in every drop.

When that bounced, my notes blamed the artist names. The second version
dropped them, along with a line calling the named person a world champion,
which I thought could read as a claim about a real person. It kept the name,
on the theory that a random name carries no public-figure risk. The third was
the fallback I had written in advance: a few lines of genre description with
the name in quotes.

The same notes already had the next move planned if the name was the problem:
generate with a placeholder word, then splice the name onto the output. That
would have put me back in the splice pipeline, on a track that was no longer
the record.

</Depth>

## The honest verdict

<Depth max={1}>

The tool that does this job is a specialized commercial one: Suno's cover and
section-replace, at ten dollars a month.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

Three tiers of the stack, each wrong in its own way:

| Stack level | Tool | Why it failed |
|---|---|---|
| General-purpose cloud | Lyria 3 Pro | Over-refuses: blocks proper nouns in vocals as a class |
| General-purpose open source | YingMusic | Under-articulates the name, out-of-distribution consonants |
| Specialized commercial | Suno cover + replace | Actually does the job, at ten dollars a month |

</Depth>

Picking the right *level* of the stack mattered more than picking the most
powerful tool, and for this task the right level was a paid product I'd been
too stubborn to start with.

## How close it actually is

<Depth min={3}>

I don't read this as a failure. YingMusic didn't exist six months
ago. The path to run diffusion audio models on Apple silicon matured this year.
Teaching the model the shouted-chant texture it's missing is plausibly a
weekend of compute, with one catch: the hard part is sourcing and
force-aligning a few hours of clean festival-vocal data, the same alignment
problem that ate the splice path. And the pipeline I hand-built
-- separate the stem, detect the onsets, synthesize the word, splice and mix --
is the kind of thing that tends to fold into a single model once someone bothers.
I'd bet on that inside a year or two. The caveat is that "just assemble the
pieces" is exactly the false-easy this evening disproved. Assembly is the hard
part, and I have a podcast-guest-over-a-house-track to prove it.

Music generation hasn't had its GPT-3 moment, the one where the thing that
took an expert a weekend becomes a text box anyone can use. But you can see all
the precursors from here. I went in to swap one name and came out with a
pretty clear map of where the edge is, which is the return on an evening
spent at it. Bleeding edge isn't the same as ready. It's just close enough to
be worth the cuts.

</Depth>
