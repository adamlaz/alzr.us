The white ASA collar that snaps around my pool's floating light began as a
pencil sketch and exactly two measurements: 110 millimeters across the light
body, 25 millimeters up the brim. Its whole job is width -- it makes the
light too wide to get pulled into the skimmer throat, a part nobody sells. I
never opened a CAD application. I described what I wanted,
Claude wrote OpenSCAD, a render showed us both what the words had actually
said, and on June 21st the first print snapped onto the light and held.

That was week one. Three weeks later the same loop had produced a drawer of
custom Gridfinity bins, a batch plate of nine hole plugs, a rod-holder insert
for a fishing buddy, and a tri-color joke sign, and it had picked up a
discipline I didn't expect to need.

## The geometry is text

OpenSCAD is CAD as source code: a part is a program, and the program compiles
to a solid.<Note id="1" /><Depth inline max={1}> A language model can't drag a
mouse across a 3D surface, but it writes and revises text fluently.</Depth><Depth inline min={2}> No sketching, no mouse, no feature tree -- a
`cylinder()` here, a `difference()` there, variables for every dimension that
might change.</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

For an AI pair, that property is the entire ballgame. A language model has no
eyes on a B-rep surface and no cursor to drag a fillet with. It does have
fluency in structured text: emitting it, revising it, holding its rules in
its head. Code-CAD converts "model this" into a text-transformation problem.<Depth inline min={3}>
And the side effects are the point: the part is parametric by default,
diffable in git, and re-generatable for as long as the library holds still.</Depth>
One dimension on the collar is still a guess -- the skimmer throat is modeled
at 225 millimeters because I haven't fished the real number out of the deck
yet -- and when I do, the fix is editing one variable and reprinting, not
remodeling.

</Depth>

The geometry was always text, of course<Depth inline min={2}>; OpenSCAD predates the AI that now
writes it by fifteen years</Depth>. What changed is who writes it. The unit of
iteration used to be my evening with a manual, and now it's a sentence. The
heavy lifting rides on BOSL2, the community geometry library that gives
OpenSCAD real fillets, edge rounding, and attachment semantics.<Note id="2" />
Claude reaches for library constructs I wouldn't have found in a week of
docs, and it also invents plausible-looking arguments that don't exist.
Which is why the whole workflow hangs on a loop.

<Depth min={4}>

The collar file is 242 lines and exposes 29 values to OpenSCAD's Customizer,
each with a comment and most with a slider range. Two of them are
measurements. The others are choices: a 4 mm wall, 0.4 mm of clearance around
the brim, five arms, a 12 mm outer ring. Every other dimension is derived from
those 29, so changing `light_d` moves the bore, the shelf, the lid opening and
the snap posts together.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The derived block, verbatim:

```scad
cavity_d     = light_d + 2 * clearance;     // bore that hugs the brim
shelf_id     = light_d - 2 * bottom_lip;    // light rests on this ring
lid_id       = light_d - 2 * top_lip;       // open hole through the lid
wall_od      = cavity_d + 2 * wall;         // OD of brim wall + lid ring
shelf_z      = flange_h;                    // top of the cup shelf
brim_top_z   = shelf_z + brim_h;            // top edge of the brim
lid_top_z    = brim_top_z + roof;           // top face of the lid (assembled)
post_inner_r = wall_od / 2 + post_gap;      // inner face of the posts
```

With the measured 110 and 25, that is a 110.8 mm bore inside a 118.8 mm hub, a
100 mm opening through the shelf, and a lid whose top face sits 33 mm above
the build plate.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

For caps, plugs, rings, shims, brackets, and bins, code-CAD driven by
conversation is now the fastest tool I own -- a claim I would not have
believed in May.

</Depth>

## How the collar holds together

The cup is three modules in one union: a hub, a frame and five snap posts. The
hub is a cylinder with the brim cavity bored out of it, leaving a shelf that
laps 5 mm under the light. The frame is a wheel 2 mm thick: the 225 mm outer
ring, 12 mm wide, joined to the hub by five 10 mm spokes, with every corner of
the 2D footprint rounded to a 4 mm fillet before it is extruded. The lid is a
single BOSL2 `tube()`, a flat ring 4 mm thick that laps 3 mm over the top of
the brim.

Each post is a cantilever that stands 0.6 mm clear of the hub wall, so the two
don't fuse on the bed, and ends in a hook that laps 3 mm over the lid. Its
root bites 2 mm into the hub wall, which is what the fifth shape of the
evening changed. The posts pull the lid down and trap the 25 mm brim between
it and the shelf.

The no-supports requirement shows up as geometry in two places. Each brim
window is a 16 mm slot topped by a gable that narrows to a point over 8 mm of
height, a 45-degree roof the printer can build with nothing under it. The
underside of each hook is a 45-degree ramp for the same reason. Three windows
sit in each gap between arms, 15 in all, and the wall under every post stays
solid.

My notes list three numbers still to lock. One is the skimmer throat width
behind `collar_od`. The second is `frame_h`, the wheel thickness, which trades
stiffness against material at 225 mm across. The third is the feel of the
snap, tuned through `hook_d` and `post_root` after a few cycles on the test
print.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The window cutter, verbatim. The `prismoid` is the gable: full window width at
its base, 0.2 mm at its peak, half the window width tall.

```scad
/* one window: vertical slot with a 45-deg gable roof (self-supporting) */
module window_cutter() {
    xc    = (cavity_d / 2 + wall_od / 2) / 2;
    depth = wall + 6;                       // through the wall, with margin
    z0    = shelf_z + win_base;
    zt    = brim_top_z - win_top - win_w / 2;
    move([xc, 0, (z0 + zt) / 2])
        cuboid([depth, win_w, zt - z0], rounding = win_round, edges = "Z");
    move([xc, 0, zt])
        prismoid(size1 = [depth, win_w], size2 = [depth, 0.2], h = win_w / 2, anchor = BOTTOM);
}
```

One `part` variable selects what the file emits: `assembled` for the picture
above, `bottom` and `lid` for the two STLs, `both_flat` to lay both out on one
plate. Curves render at `$fn = 160`. The two exports:

```sh
openscad -o cup.stl -D 'part="bottom"' collar.scad
openscad -o lid.stl -D 'part="lid"' collar.scad
```

</Depth>

## Compiles clean, fails as an object

Render-and-eyeball exists because the model reliably produces geometry that
compiles and still fails as an object.<Depth inline min={2}> Its recurring failure class is
composition: parts that meet but don't join.</Depth> The drawer-organizer bins made
it a trilogy, three versions in two days, every one of them passing the
renders and failing in the slicer.

<Depth min={2} max={2}>

The deep one was version 3: visible steps where the finger dips met the rim,
because the rim was composited from separate segments meeting at tangent
seams. The fix that stuck was to rebuild the rim as one closed path swept in a
single pass, dips included, so no junction exists to fail. The rule went into
the project notes as *make seams impossible instead of patching them*.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

Version 2.1: the interior corner fillets notched the rolled rim by a
millimeter at each corner, because the rim bead only covered the midline of
the wall. Version 3 was the deep one: visible steps where the finger dips met
the rim. Root cause, the rim was composited from separate segments meeting at
tangent seams -- the same "pieces pretending to be a surface" failure, now
load-bearing. The fix that stuck was categorical: rebuild the rim as one
closed 3D path swept in a single BOSL2 `path_sweep`, dips included, so no
junction exists to fail. The rule went into the project notes as *make seams
impossible instead of patching them*. Version 3.1 was the epilogue: the new
rounder body left a crescent of foot poking proud at each bottom corner,
fixed by lofting the corner radius from 3.75 up to 6 over the first 11
millimeters of height. Bounding-box checks to a hundredth of a millimeter
proved each rebuild matched the intended envelope -- code equivalence, not
print precision.

</Depth>

## What each slicer catch changed

The stock Gridfinity generator only makes boxes, so the bin bodies were custom
from version 2 on: a rolled rim, a finger dip in each end wall, a filleted
interior, and feet built to the published Gridfinity profile so they seat in
the plates like stock bins.

Version 2 ran one bead along the midline of a 2 mm wall. The interior corner
fillets made the wall wider than 2 mm at the corners, and the bead didn't
cover the extra. Version 2.1 added a cap that followed the wall and the
fillets, which held the crest at 42.00 mm all the way around. The rim was now
three kinds of piece, a swept bead, the cap and separate arcs for the dips,
each relying on exact tangency where it met the next.

Version 3 threw that out. One closed 3D path now runs the whole rim, and one
circle is swept along it. Two supporting changes made a single bead
sufficient. The wall became a uniform 2 mm: outer corners at a 6 mm radius,
with the interior corner fillet and the floor fillet both 4 mm and cut by the
same sphere, so 6 minus the wall is exactly 4 and no corner is fatter than the
bead. And the dips changed from circular arcs to a cosine curve 56 mm wide and
13 mm deep, which meets the straight rim with zero slope, so no crease marks
where a dip ends.

Version 3.1 was a consequence of version 3. The body now had 6 mm corners and
the feet kept the Gridfinity profile's 3.75 mm, which left about 0.9 mm of
foot showing at each bottom corner. The body's corner radius now starts at
3.75 where it meets the foot and reaches 6 at a height of 11 mm, where the
interior fillet is fully developed. The straight faces stay vertical and the
corners ease out at about 8.5 degrees. The feet didn't change, because by then
the printed grid had already proven the socket fit.

After each rebuild the STL bounding boxes were checked against the math: 83.5
mm wide, 167.5, 209.5 or 251.5 mm long depending on the model, 42.00 mm tall.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

<CadConvRimProfile />

The whole rim is these lines. The path follows the wall midline, gets
resampled so no segment is longer than 0.6 mm, and drops by `dipz` only on the
two end walls:

```scad
function dipz(x) = abs(x) < dip_hw ? dip_d / 2 * (1 + cos(180 * x / dip_hw)) : 0;

rim_mid_2d = subdivide_path(
    rect([ox - wall, oy - wall], rounding = r_body - bead_r),
    maxlen = 0.6, closed = true);

rim_path = [for (p = rim_mid_2d)
    [p.x, p.y,
     wall_top - (abs(p.y) > (oy - wall) / 2 - 0.01 ? dipz(p.x) : 0)]];

module rim_bead() {
    path_sweep(circle(r = bead_r), rim_path, closed = true);
}
```

Order matters in the assembly. The dip cutters come out of the walls first and
the bead is unioned last, so the bead caps the cut edge:

```scad
union() {
    difference() {
        union() {
            feet();
            body_shell();
        }
        cavity();
        dip_cutter();
    }
    rim_bead();
}
```

The version 3.1 fix is a `hull()` between two thin slices, one with the foot's
corner radius and one with the body's:

```scad
module body_shell() {
    hull() {
        translate([0, 0, 4.74]) linear_extrude(0.01) rrect(ox, oy, 3.75);
        translate([0, 0, blend_top]) linear_extrude(0.01) rrect(ox, oy, r_body);
    }
    translate([0, 0, blend_top])
        linear_extrude(wall_top - blend_top) rrect(ox, oy, r_body);
}
```

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**The rod-holder insert** -- a gift for a fishing buddy -- dropped the sketch
entirely: modeled from three reference photos and four parametric inputs, an
Assassin-style tapered cushion that wedges into an oversized boat rod holder.
Its keyhole slot has to flex open to clip over a rigged rod, which drove the
material call (stiff filament cracks at the slot's neck unless you give up
the snap). And it carries my favorite trick of the batch: the model ships a
`part="cutaway"` half-section whose only job is letting both of us see the
internal bore and slot in a render -- an inspection fixture, living in the
same file as the part.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Four inputs drive the whole insert: the rod shaft diameter, the cone's
diameter at its wide top and at its narrow base, and its height. The printed
one is 10, 51, 40 and 60 mm. The bore is the rod plus 0.4 mm of clearance on
each side, 10.8 mm, and the slot neck is 90 percent of the bore, which pinches
a 10 mm rod by 0.28 mm on its way in. My notes say to drop that factor toward
0.8 if the rod doesn't hold, and to set it to 1.0 for rigid PLA: an open slot
with no snap. For the snap version they name PETG or TPU 95A.

It prints narrow end down with no supports. The six grip flutes around the top
are cut by vertical cylinders placed at the top radius, so they bite only
where the cone is wide and fade out above the zone that wedges into the
holder.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**The sign.** A friend named Doug was always going to receive a "BEWARE of
DOUG" sign in the style of the classic black-and-red enamel original, and
multi-color is where slicer craft starts to matter. The
lettering lives in a one-millimeter front skin -- about five layers -- so the
material swaps stay cheap, purge waste dumps into the big black body as
infill, and the sign prints face down with the text pre-mirrored so the flush
"inlay" face comes off the plate looking screen-printed.

It also surfaced the best gotcha of the month: OpenSCAD's fast preview
mis-colors intersected geometry, so the field flashed red until we forced the
full CGAL render. The fast picture lies a little.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The sign is 150 by 105 mm and 3 mm thick. The body is solid black from top to
bottom and the white and red exist only in the skin, so at a 0.20 mm layer
height the printer swaps filament across five layers and prints the other ten
in one color. The file exports as a single 3MF with the three colors baked in
per region, with three STLs on shared coordinates as the fallback.

The mis-colored preview has a specific cause. The rounded outer edge comes
from intersecting every color region with one rounded envelope, and
OpenSCAD's fast preview draws that intersection in the wrong color. The
`--render` flag on the command line, or F6 in the app, computes the exact
geometry per color.

The positions of "of" and "DOUG" come from glyph widths measured once and
typed into the file as constants, so it opens in any OpenSCAD build without
turning on an experimental text-measuring feature.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The slicer settings my notes recommend for the sign:

| Setting | Value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Layer height | 0.20 mm, or 0.16 for sharper letters | the color band is 1.0 mm, five layers at 0.20 |
| Orientation | inlay face down, raised face up | both are the as-imported orientation |
| Walls / top / bottom | 3 / 4 / 4 | clean show faces |
| Infill | 15% gyroid | it's a plaque |
| Supports | off | nothing overhangs |
| Flush into objects and infill | on | sends the purge into the black body |
| Prime tower | on | required for the swaps, and small here |

**The hole plugs** replaced a 290-piece assortment of stick-on hole covers
whose drawer never once contained the size I needed. The replacement is a

domed head, optional retention ribs, and a batch layout that grids out

<Depth min={4}>

The generator takes a hole diameter, a wall thickness, a head scale, a head
thickness and a plug depth. At the file's defaults the head is 1.25 times the
hole diameter and 0.8 mm thick, the plug wall is 1 mm, and the retention ribs
come from a sawtooth profile stepped every 0.5 mm. The whole plug is one 2D
profile revolved around its axis, with an optional slot cut through the shank.
The nine sizes are 8.5, 8.8, 9, 10.5, 10.8, 11, 13.5, 14 and 15 mm: three
groups of three.

</Depth>

**The drawer kit** ran the loop at furniture scale.<Depth inline max={2}> The grid needed no
modeling, because the Gridfinity project already ships a baseplate generator.
Custom work started only at the bins, and those are the three slicer failures
above.</Depth><Depth inline min={3}> The Gridfinity project
already ships a parametric baseplate generator, so the grid itself needed

256-millimeter bed. An eight-by-ten grid of 42-millimeter units covers 336

<CadConvDrawerPlates />

Two generator calls made the grid. Both ask for a 4 by 5 plate in the thin 5
mm style with magnet holes off. The rear call adds a target length of 239 mm
and a flag that pushes all 29 mm of padding to one edge. Two copies of each
cover the drawer, one plate per print job, and the grid pitch continues across
the seams, so a bin can sit astride two plates. The first job was one front
plate plus a single-cell test bin, checked in the drawer before the other
three plates ran. Everything prints in PLA on the stock 0.20 mm profile with
no supports.

The bin kit is three models, all two cells wide and 42 mm tall with no
stacking lip: four 2x5 bins, two 2x6 and two 2x4. Those eight bins cover all
80 cells in four columns, and three bins fit the bed side by side, so the kit
is three print jobs.

The fork decides one column. A 2x5 bin is about 205 mm long inside at the top
and 198 at the floor. If the longest dinner fork runs over 200 mm, the fork
column flips to a 2x6 and a 2x4: the same models at different print counts,
with nothing to re-render. A knife or serving piece over 245 mm means
rendering a 2x7 and pairing it with a 2x3 to keep the column at ten cells.

The pad strip got a part too. A rail 28.5 mm deep and 37.2 mm tall sits on the
pad behind the bins, in two pieces because 335 mm won't fit the bed even on
the diagonal. The long piece keeps a slot about 251 mm long for chopsticks and
skewers. Its height is arithmetic: bins nest 4.75 mm into the 5 mm plates and
top out 42.25 mm off the drawer floor, and the rail on its 5 mm pad lands at
42.2. The first rail missed that and stood 4.75 mm proud of the bins until a
polish pass the same day caught it.

Printed solid, the fifteen pieces would weigh about 1.8 kg. My notes budget
1.2 to 1.4 kg once the slicer applies sparse infill, with the bins alone
landing around a full 1 kg spool.

The grid is two calls to the stock baseplate generator, plus one to the stock
bin generator for the test bin. From the build script, with paths shortened:

```sh
# style_plate=0 -> thin plate (5mm, the drawer style). No magnets in a drawer.
common=(-D style_plate=0 -D enable_magnet=false)

openscad -o baseplate-front-4x5.stl \
  -D gridx=4 -D gridy=5 "${common[@]}" \
  "$REPO/gridfinity-rebuilt-baseplate.scad"

# fity=1 pushes the 29mm pad to one edge -- that edge goes against the back wall.
openscad -o baseplate-rear-4x5-pad.stl \
  -D gridx=4 -D gridy=5 -D distancey=239 -D fity=1 "${common[@]}" \
  "$REPO/gridfinity-rebuilt-baseplate.scad"

# Socket fit check before designing custom bins. Plain 1x1x3u, flat base, no tab.
openscad -o test-bin-1x1x3.stl \
  -D gridx=1 -D gridy=1 -D gridz=3 \
  -D refined_holes=false -D style_tab=5 -D scoop=0 \
  "$REPO/gridfinity-rebuilt-bins.scad"

for len in 4 5 6; do
  openscad -o "bin-2x${len}.stl" -D gy="$len" cutlery-bin.scad
done
```

The script ends by printing the sizes every STL should measure: plates 168 x
210 x 5 and 168 x 239 x 5, the test bin 41.5 square, bins 83.5 x 167.5, 209.5
or 251.5 x 42, rails 255 and 80 x 28.5 x 37.2. It needs OpenSCAD's snapshot
build, whose Manifold backend renders each plate in seconds.

<Depth min={3}>

Rules the month wrote into the project notes: search the model libraries
before modeling anything (the printer's own desiccant containers were a
download, not a design); no calipers means no measuring, and an unmeasured
part waits; material follows exposure, ASA where the Florida sun and pool
water get a vote, flexible where a neck has to bend, plain PLA indoors.
Everything prints on a Bambu Lab X2D, whose heated chamber is the spec doing
quiet work here -- it's what makes ASA a routine print instead of a gamble.
And the bottleneck is no longer modeling. Two of the five builds sit
gated on a number only I can go get, which makes the critical path of the
whole workflow me, holding a tape measure.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The search rule has an order in my notes: the printer maker's own model
library first, because its files carry print profiles, then three other
public libraries. The material rule has a number behind it. PLA starts to
warp at about 55°C, which is why a closed car or full Florida sun destroys
it, and the printer's chamber heats to 65°C, which is why it prints ASA
comfortably. Flexible filament
feeds from an external spool, because flexibles jam the multi-material unit.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

## The inputs keep getting thinner

Look at the input column again: a sketch, then photos, then a single
reference image, then bare numbers off a tape measure. The loop holds as the
input thins: three photos or two tape-measure numbers still come back as a
shape, and judging the shape is the same job at every thickness.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The month started in FreeCAD: I hand-modeled replacement card-table inserts
and a solar-light plate one feature at a time with a mouse. Real parts, fine
work -- but the skill that compounded was mine alone, locked in my hands.
The conversation loop compounds in a file: the collar taught render
checking, the bins taught seam discipline, the sign taught color economy,
and each rule went into the project notes the next session reads first.

The collar ships with this post: the
[OpenSCAD source](/models/cad-by-conversation/skimmer-light-collar.scad),
plus the print-ready
[cup](/models/cad-by-conversation/skimmer-light-collar-cup.stl) and
[lid](/models/cad-by-conversation/skimmer-light-collar-lid.stl) STLs. Flat,
support-free, ASA recommended if it lives outside.

</Depth>

The collar's first print has been snapping on and off the pool light since
June while one last number decides its final width. The sketch and every
taste call were mine; the geometry was negotiated. I still can't sculpt a
solid with a mouse. The scarce skill is knowing what right looks like when
it renders, and being willing to say "again" until it does.<Note id="4" />
