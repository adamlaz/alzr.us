Ninety-two applications landed in the second hour. We posted four roles one
afternoon, and 32 hours later we had 816, about 770 of them distinct. My first
thought was that everyone else must be using AI to apply. My second was that
I'd better step up my game.

<ArrivalsChart />

The volume was real: the engineering role alone beat our busiest earlier
posting, and all four together drew about twenty times what a typical posting
gets in its first day and a half. When I checked the text for AI tells, though,
the numbers went the other way.

<AiTellsChart />

<Depth min={2}>

Median intro length barely moved, from 71 words on earlier postings to 72 now.
No two strangers share even a fifth of their six-word phrases. About 1% of
applicants visibly used AI, either by saying so or by addressing an AI reader.

</Depth>

You can't spot AI in the application text anymore. We built the form around
that from the start by asking for something we can verify.

<Depth min={2}>

The four roles span engineering, design and product, and each one asks for the
same profile: someone who has done one craft well and loved it, and who uses AI
to cover the other two. The single-hat role is fading.

My earlier posts covered the two hiring stages that come after this one:
background research with parallel Claude Code agents and the interview that
follows.<Note id="1" /> Both started from a pile I could read myself. At 816
applications, I needed a pass in front of them to decide who to read first.

</Depth>

## Asking what the AI got wrong

The form asks: *What did the AI get wrong, and how did you catch it?* Half of
the 765 applications scored so far landed at 2 out of 4 -- some version of "AI
makes mistakes, so I always review." Only 21 earned a 4 by naming a specific
failure, explaining how they caught it, and linking something to show for it.

<AiScoresChart />

<Depth min={2}>

That question is the job: the AI got something wrong, and a person caught it.
An assistant can invent that story and fake a file to match, but it can't walk
me through the fix when I say "open it and show me" on the call. In the
interviews, a 1 on AI fluency ends a candidacy.

</Depth>

## Fifteen cents an application

Reading all 816 by hand would have taken 27 to 54 hours: two to four minutes
each for a resume, three short answers and a link, before a single phone call.
So the night the roles went live, I had agents build a first pass.

<Depth min={3}>

<BuildNightTimeline />

Before building anything new, the agents found that our existing nightly
research job had gone blind: the role labels in the candidate database had
changed, so the job no longer recognized two of the four roles. After fixing
that and building the triage pass, the first scoring run made 1,080 model calls
with zero failures.

</Depth>

Scripts check the links and public work each applicant sent. Then a model
reads each application three separate times against the role's rubric and
sorts it into one of three piles: research (a deeper look before any call),
maybe, or likely pass. A fresh scoring run costs about fifteen cents an
application in model time<Depth inline min={4}>: three Opus 5.5 samples at
about five cents each</Depth>.

<PilesChart />

<Depth min={2}>

We run three reads and take a vote because a single model pass is
noisy.<Note id="2" /> Two of the three have to agree before a signal counts.

</Depth>

I read one row per applicant: the pile, a strip of flags, a short note leading
with the strongest evidence and what to probe, and a first question for the

## A hunch can't reject anyone

The AI only sorts and flags. It can't advance or reject anyone, and a model's
suspicion alone can't even put an applicant in the bottom pile.<Depth inline max={1}> I go through the piles and make every call.</Depth>

That rule came from testing the model against our own past work. The same
night, before running it on the new wave, I had it score 91 earlier applicants
whose backgrounds we had already researched. Seven times, the model decided on
its own that an application didn't add up -- catching four applicants our
research had flagged as fake, and misflagging three who were real.

<CalibrationChart />

<Depth min={2}>

Putting someone in likely pass requires a problem a script verified, two
clearly missed must-haves, or a craft score of 1. A model suspicion on its own
adds a look-closer flag, a small sort penalty and a question for the call.

</Depth>

A handful of applicants addressed an AI
reader directly in their answers. None of them talked their way into a higher
pile.

</Depth>

<PullQuote attribution="From the guide my teammates interview with">
  One flag means look closer. It never means reject.

The other guardrails:

- The code strips names, contact details, schools and cities before the model
  reads anything, though a review the next day found gaps.
- The script drops any claim the model can't back with an exact quote<Depth inline min={4}>:
  twelve words or fewer, matched word for word against the source so the model
  can't invent quotes</Depth>.
- Scoring ignores instructions addressed to an AI and quotes them in the note
  for me to see.
- Only a person can change a candidate's status, and the write path checks
  every update to enforce it.

## Where I overruled the agents

Agents drafted the job postings, the scoring rubric and the triage code,
pushing for their own defaults along the way. I overruled them on six calls.

<OverruleBoard />

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The pay range is the one I got wrong. I initially left it off; an hour later,
before the postings went live, the agents flagged that several states require a
range on remote listings, so I put it on.

The AI-signal call is the one I'd defend hardest. The first draft treated
unusual uses of AI in an application as integrity problems and penalized them in
the sort. I told the agent to surface them without a penalty: "I don't know
what I want to do with that signal, but I would like to call it out so that as
we go through more candidates, I can figure out if it's a positive or a
negative signal." And: "At the very least, I think that's a signal that
they're using or aware of AI." We're hiring people who use AI well; penalizing
awareness of it would have buried strong applicants.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

When I asked the agent afterward to assess that call, it wrote: "I was
optimizing to catch cheaters with a signal that hadn't been shown to mean
anything. He wanted to measure it first."

</Depth>

## A second model checked the first one's work

The next day I had a different model, Fable 5.2, review the whole pipeline
cold. It read the code, the saved run artifacts and the live rows in read-only
mode, with no context from the session that built the system. It came back with
one must-fix and seven should-fix findings, several of them already affecting
live rows.

The must-fix was a check meant to link related applications. It matched on
values that thousands of unrelated applicants share, so strangers looked
connected and dropped in the sort. One applicant who met four of five
must-haves sat near the bottom partly because of it.

The sharpest catch was in the step that strips names and places before scoring.
Instead of taking the docs' word for it, the reviewer opened every saved model
packet from that first night and counted what had actually gone into the prompt.

<RedactionChart />

Most of the leaked names weren't in the application text at all. They came from
facts the scripts appended afterward, like a portfolio domain built from a
first and last name, which the redaction step never ran on. The reviewer
found the opposite bug too: an overly broad rule for stripping school names had
wiped five intros and one AI answer blank.

The 6% of name matches remaining after the fix are mostly applicants writing
their own name inside an answer, or name parts that collide with common English
words.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3} max={3}>

The fix didn't reach most of the live scores yet. A cache bug kept the pre-fix
reads on 505 applications, so I'm re-running those before I start reviewing.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The fix didn't reach most of the live scores yet. While I was drafting this
post, a reader agent checking my arithmetic noticed the run totals didn't add
up. The agents had keyed the sample cache on the application ID instead of the redacted
packet, so the re-run after the redaction fix reused cached reads from the
leaky packets on 505 applications. It recomputed their piles and sort order
under the new rules while leaving the old model reads underneath. Keying the
cache on the packet itself and re-reading those 505 takes under an hour of
model time before I start reviewing.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

None of these bugs tripped the builder agent's own tests, which only
checked the cases the builder thought to write. Auditing the saved packets on
disk caught what the tests missed.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Four of the seven should-fix findings were silent failures:

- A failed lookup counted as a missing link, so one expired token would have
  flagged every applicant.
- A capitalized `Https://` registered as a dead link.
- The nightly job only compared each night's arrivals against each other
  instead of against the full pile.
- Hiding duplicate applications could hide the row where I had already recorded
  a decision.

The second night surfaced one more false link of the same kind as the must-fix,
tying two strangers together on a common field. I fixed the check and corrected
both rows by hand.

## Inside the scoring run

The pipeline separates deterministic checks from model reads so the scoring
model never touches the web or holds any tools. Scripts verify links and record
each result as a plain fact a person can check; a separate, narrower check
opens the work each applicant linked. The scoring model only sees the redacted
packet: the application text plus those recorded facts.

Each of the three reads is a fresh headless `claude -p` call with no tools, an
empty working directory, no saved session, the role's rubric as the system
prompt, and output constrained to a JSON schema. The schema requires a verdict
and verbatim quote for each must-have, a craft score, an AI-answer score,
quoted signals, a suggested lane for the product roles, a tier and a one-line
note.

To combine the three reads, craft and AI scores take the median, each must-have
takes the majority, and the tier takes the plurality, with ties going to maybe.
If two reads ever disagree by two or more points, or one votes research while
another votes likely pass, the row keeps the median and adds a flag for me to
inspect. Across 812 applications, no row triggered that split flag; the three
reads agreed unanimously on the tier 92% of the time and split 2-1 on the rest.

Caching every sample on disk makes adding deterministic checks cheap. Running a
new set of public checks across all 812 applications cost $1.42 in model time
because only the nine newest arrivals needed fresh model reads; the other 803
just picked up the new findings as flags. That is the same cache that held onto
the pre-fix reads for 505 rows.

Because the machine had no API key configured, the runner calls `claude -p`
against a subscription six at a time instead of using the Batch API. That costs
about twice what Batch would (fifteen cents an application instead of roughly
eight), in exchange for watching runs finish live at about nine applications a
minute. Across two days, the wave ran about 2,500 model calls with zero
failures.

<RunCostsChart />

We haven't run a bias audit yet. Redaction targets names, schools and cities;
employers, graduation years and writing style stay in the packet. The prompt
tells the model never to let name, origin, age, gender, location or school
affect a score, which is an instruction, not a guarantee.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The vote and the guard, as pseudocode:

```python
def triage(application, rubric):
    facts   = run_checks(application)             # deterministic; a person can verify each
    packet  = redact(application) + facts         # targets names, contacts, schools, cities
    samples = [score(packet, rubric, schema=SCHEMA) for _ in range(3)]

    for s in samples:                             # evidence or abstain
        s.drop_claims_not_quoted_verbatim(packet, application)

    craft   = median(s.craft for s in samples)
    ai      = median(s.ai_answer for s in samples)
    tier    = plurality([s.tier for s in samples], tie="maybe")
    signals = [sig for sig in reported_signals(samples) if votes(sig) >= 2]

    # Suspicion alone can never send someone to the bottom pile.
    if tier == "likely_pass" and not (facts.checked_problems
                                      or missed_must_haves(samples) >= 2
                                      or craft == 1):
        tier = "maybe"

    rank = (weighted(craft, ai, must_haves(samples))
            - penalties(facts.checked_problems)
            - small_penalty(model_suspicions(samples)))
    return tier, rank, flags(facts, signals), note(samples)


def write(row, fields):
    assert "Status" not in fields                 # every caller goes through here; a person moves Status
    db.update(row, fields)
```

## Reading the 124 first

Instead of starting at the top of 816 raw applications, I start with 124
research notes -- two to four hours of reading at a minute or two each, with
the 660 maybes sorted behind them so the strongest are on top. That order is an
estimate and a bet: I'll only know whether those 124 were the right 124 once
I've made real decisions.

<ReadingHoursChart />

<Depth min={3}>

The first calibration check wasn't encouraging. Measured against the research
agents' earlier verdicts on those 91 past applicants rather than hiring
outcomes, the sort did only modestly better than a coin flip, at an AUC of
0.63. The earlier form didn't ask the AI question, the strongest input now, so
this wave may sort differently. We label every triage field uncalibrated until we
have fifty human decisions on this wave to measure against.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The sort also can't see its own misses. Decisions only happen on applications I
read, so nothing measures the bottom of the maybe pile unless I sample it on
purpose.

</Depth>

Having the research and triage notes ready before anyone talks to a candidate
also lets teammates run the deeper interviews right after my first call instead
of waiting on me.

<Depth min={2}>

They don't need to be practiced interviewers. The research hands them
questions written for that candidate, and a short guide times the hour.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The guide takes about ten minutes to read and sets a fixed pre-read order: the
AI answer first, then the triage note, then the research. During the hour-long
deep dive, the candidate spends fifteen minutes walking us through their work
and later makes one small change live with their usual tools so we can watch
how they work. Everyone scores independently before the group discusses. From
the guide: "Someone who made the work can explain any piece of it. Someone who
didn't, can't. You'll notice." Pay conversations stay with me.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The guide also includes a table of questions we never ask, with
innocent-sounding examples like "What year did you graduate?", and what to do
if something feels off: ask them to open their own work and change something
small, never confront, and never treat an accent, a name, a time zone or a quiet online
presence as a sign of anything.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The work sample mirrors the roles: three to five hours across a week, one
piece in the applicant's own craft and one outside it. Structured interviews
and work samples have predicted job performance better than most methods for
decades, and a 2022 re-analysis that brought most of the numbers down still
put structured interviews on top.<Note id="3" /> Triage just gets us to those
conversations faster.
