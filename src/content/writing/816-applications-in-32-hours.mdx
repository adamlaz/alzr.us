Ninety-two applications landed in the second hour. We posted four roles one
afternoon, and 32 hours later there were 816 applications, about 770 of them
distinct. My first thought was that everyone else must be using AI to apply.
My second was that I'd better step up my game.

Then I checked. The pace was real: about twice our previous record, and ten
times what a normal posting gets in its first day and a half. The AI tells
went the other way. There were fewer of them than on our earlier postings.

<Depth min={2}>

Em dashes showed up in 14% of intros on our earlier postings and 5% on these.
Stock phrases like "resonates" and "at the intersection of" fell from 29% of
intros to 17%. Median intro length didn't move. No two strangers share even a
fifth of their phrasing. Applicants who visibly used AI, by saying so or by
addressing an AI reader, came to about 1%.

</Depth>

You can't see AI in the text anymore. The form was already built for that: it
asks for something we can check.

<Depth max={1}>

Its key question: *What did the AI get wrong, and how did you catch it?*
Only 21 of about 770 got full marks for a specific story, with how they caught
it and something to show for it.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The roles span engineering, design and product, and all four ask for the same
kind of person: someone who has done one craft well and loved it, and who uses
AI to cover the other two. The single-hat role is fading. The application form
tests for what replaces it with one question: *What did the AI get wrong, and
how did you catch it?*

I've written about the two hiring stages that come after this one: background
research with parallel Claude Code agents, and the interview that follows
it.<Note id="1" /> Both started from a pile I could read myself. This is the
stage before either, sorting who I look at first, when the pile arrives faster
than anyone can read it.

</Depth>

Reading all 816 the old way would have cost 27 to 54 hours: two to four
minutes each for a resume, three short answers and a link, before a single
phone call. So the night the roles went live, I had agents build a first pass.

<Depth min={2}>

## Fifteen cents an application

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

I asked for it at 7:24 that evening, with about 300 applications already in.
The first thing the agents found was that the nightly research job had gone
blind: the role labels in the candidate database had changed, and the job no
longer recognized two of the four. By 10:15 I'd made the calls the agents
needed from me. By 11:16 the first 360 applications were scored: 1,080 model
calls, zero failures, 41 minutes.

</Depth>

Scripts check the links and public work each applicant gave us. Then a model
reads the application three separate times against the role's rubric and
sorts it into a pile with a short note: research (a deeper look before any
call), maybe, or likely pass. Of the 812 sorted by the end of the second day,
124 landed in the research pile, 660 in maybe and 28 in likely pass. It cost
about fifteen cents an application in model time<Depth inline min={4}>: three
Opus 5.5 samples at about five cents each, and about $143 in reported cost
for everything over the two days, public-work checks included</Depth>.

Here's a composite, not a real applicant, to show the whole path. A product
manager applies with a link to a metrics write-up and an AI story: an
AI-written query double-counted refunds, and they caught it when the totals
didn't match the finance report. A check confirms the link is live and the
write-up is theirs. All three reads agree on strong craft and a specific AI
story, with two of the role's must-haves still unproven. They land in the
research pile with a ✅ next to the verified link and a note that says to ask
how they'd size a feature. My first question on the call is already written:
show me that query, and what you changed after the totals didn't match.

<Depth min={2}>

Three reads and a vote, because one model read is a noisy
measurement.<Note id="2" /> Two of three have to agree before a signal counts.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Every claim the model makes about an application has to quote it, word for
word, in twelve words or fewer. Code checks each quote against the original.
A claim without a real quote gets thrown out, so the model can't invent
evidence for its own opinion.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

What I read is one row per applicant: a number to sort by, a note that leads
with the strongest evidence and names what to probe, and a strip of symbols.
The flags lead with symbols, not colors, because I can't reliably tell red
from green.

| Symbol | Means |
| --- | --- |
| ✅ | Checked by a script, and a good sign |
| ⚠️ | Checked by a script, and a problem |
| 🔎 | Look closer. Never decides a pile on its own |
| ℹ️ | Worth knowing |

## What did the AI get wrong?

Only 21 of the 765 applicants scored so far got full marks on *What did the
AI get wrong, and how did you catch it?* Half scored a 2 out of 4: some version of "AI makes
mistakes, so I always review."

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The 4s share a shape. They name the mistake, say how they noticed, and link
something you can open. A typical 2, written as a composite: "AI can make
mistakes, so I always review its output carefully and test edge cases before
anything ships." It's true of everyone, and there's nothing to open.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The question is the job in one sentence: the AI was wrong, and the person
noticed. An assistant can invent that story, and it can fake a file too. It
can't walk me through it when I say "open it and show me" on the call. By
design it's the question that matters most. In the interviews, a 1 on AI
fluency ends a candidacy.

## A hunch can't reject anyone

</Depth>

One rule matters more than the speed. The AI sorts and flags, and that's all.
It can't move anyone forward or turn anyone away, and its suspicion alone
can't even put someone in the bottom pile. I go through the piles and make
every call.

<Depth min={2}>

I learned the suspicion part from the model. The same night, before trusting
it on new applicants, I had triage score 91 earlier applicants whose
backgrounds we'd already researched, so I could check its work. When the
model alone decided something didn't add up, it was right about four fake
applicants and wrong about three real people.

<PullQuote>
  An assistant can invent the story. It can't walk me through it when I say
  "open it and show me."
</PullQuote>

So the model's suspicion can no longer put anyone in likely pass. That pile
needs a problem a script checked, two clearly missed must-haves, or a craft
score of 1, and it doesn't reject anyone either. A suspicion by itself gets a
🔎, a small penalty in the sort and a question for the call.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The guard earns its keep. Of the 28 in likely pass, 21 have a problem a script
checked, mostly answers too thin to judge, and 7 rest on clearly missed
must-haves. Another 27 applications got a likely-pass vote from the model with
nothing to back it, and the guard held them at maybe. It overruled the model
about as often as it agreed with it.

Ten applications addressed an AI reader directly. Nobody talked their way up.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

A sort is still a judgment about who gets read first. That's why a hunch only
moves someone a few places, and why the bottom pile needs evidence.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The rest of the guardrails:

- The code strips names, contact details, schools and cities before the model
  reads anything. A later review found gaps.
- Any claim the model can't back with an exact quote is dropped.
- Anything in an application addressed to an AI is ignored by the scoring and
  quoted in the note for me to see.
- Odd AI-related signals stay neutral until I learn what they mean.
- Only a person changes a candidate's status, and the code enforces it.

## Where I overruled the agents

Agents drafted the postings, the scoring rubric and the triage, and they
recommended plenty along the way. These are the calls I made differently:

| The agents recommended | I decided |
| --- | --- |
| Pin the engineer to one system | Productive across all of them. The lane depends on the person |
| Wait a week to post | Post as soon as the drafts were ready |
| Show the pay range | Leave it off. Before anything went live, put it on |
| Split the reading: someone else takes the product pile | I read every role |
| Penalize odd AI-related signals | Show them, keep them neutral, learn what they mean |
| Cap nightly background research at four candidates | No cap. My time is the scarce thing, not compute |

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The pay range is the one I got wrong. The agents recommended showing it and I
said no. An hour later, well before anything was posted, they flagged that
several states expect a range on remote postings, and I put it on.

The AI-signal call is the one I'd defend hardest. The first version treated
some unusual uses of AI in an application as integrity problems, and they cost
rank. I said to show them and stay neutral: "I don't know what I want to do
with that signal, but I would like to call it out so that as we go through
more candidates, I can figure out if it's a positive or a negative signal." And:
"At the very least, I think that's a signal that they're using or aware of
AI." These roles exist to hire people who use AI well, and a penalty on
awareness of AI would have sunk some strong applicants.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The agent's own read of that call, when asked: "I was optimizing to catch
cheaters with a signal that hadn't been shown to mean anything. He wanted to
measure it first."

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

## A second model checked the first one's work

The next day I had a different model, Fable 5.2, review the whole pipeline
cold. It read the code, the saved run artifacts and the live rows, read-only,
with no context from the session that built it. It came back with one
must-fix and seven should-fix findings, and several were already live on real
rows.

The must-fix was a check for related applications. It was matching on things
thousands of unrelated applicants share, so strangers looked related and lost
rank. One applicant who met four of five must-haves sat near the bottom partly
because of it.

The best catch was in the step meant to hide names and places before scoring.
The reviewer didn't take the docs' word for it. Every run saves the exact
packet the model saw, so it opened every saved packet from that first night,
367 of them, and searched each one for
pieces of that applicant's own name and for a "City, ST" pattern. A piece of
the name survived in 35% of them, and a location in 86%. Most name leaks
weren't in the application at all. They came from facts the scripts added
afterward, like a portfolio domain built from a first and last name, which the
redaction step had never covered. It found the opposite failure too: a rule
for removing schools wiped five intros and one AI answer completely.

The same count after the fix: a piece of the name in 7% of packets, mostly in
applicants' own answers and some of them just common words, and no city lines
at all.

The fix didn't reach most of the scores, though. While I was writing this post,
a reader agent checking my arithmetic noticed the run numbers didn't add up. The
sample cache was keyed on the application, not on what the model actually
read, so the re-score after the fix reused the old reads for 505 applications,
reads taken on the leaky packets. Their piles and sort scores were recomputed
under the new rules; the reads underneath weren't. The fix is to key the cache
on the input itself and re-read those 505, under an hour of model time,
before I start reviewing.

None of this showed up in the builder's own tests, which checked that the code
did what it was written to do. The reviewer counted what the model actually
received, and that only works because every packet gets saved.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Four of the seven should-fixes failed silently:

- A failed lookup counted as a missing link, so one expired token would have
  flagged every applicant.
- A capitalized `Https://` read as a dead link.
- The nightly run only compared each night's arrivals with each other, never
  with the whole pile.
- Hiding duplicates could hide the row I'd already decided on.

The second night turned up one more of the same kind as the must-fix: two
strangers looked linked over something thousands of people share. Fixed, and
I corrected both rows by hand.

## Two layers, three reads, one vote

A deterministic layer runs the checks and writes each result down as a fact a
person could verify. A model layer reads a redacted packet, the application
plus those facts, and scores it against the role's rubric. The scoring model
never touches the web and has no tools; a separate, narrower check opens the
work each applicant linked.

Each of the three samples is a fresh headless `claude -p` run: no tools, an
empty working directory, no saved session, the rubric as the system prompt,
and output forced to a JSON schema. The schema holds a verdict and a quote for
each must-have, a craft score, an AI-answer score, signals with quotes, a
suggested lane for the product roles, a tier and a one-line note.

The vote: craft and AI scores take the median of three, each must-have takes
the majority, a signal needs two of three samples with verified quotes, and
the tier is the plurality, with ties going to maybe. When samples disagree by
two or more points, or one says research while another says likely pass, the
row gets the median as well as a flag for a person. Across 812 applications,
no row tripped that flag. The three reads agreed on the tier 92% of the time
and split 2-1 on the rest. That 8% is what the extra two reads pay for.

Every sample is cached on disk, which makes adding checks cheap: running a new
set of public checks across all 812 cost $1.42 in model time, because only the
nine newest applications needed model reads. The other 803 got the new
findings as flags. It's the same cache that kept the leaky reads for 505.

It runs on headless `claude -p` against a subscription instead of the Batch
API, six at a time, because there was no API key on the machine. That costs
about twice what Batch would (fifteen cents an application against roughly
eight), and in exchange I can watch every run synchronously, at about nine
applications a minute. About 2,500 model calls for this wave over two days,
zero failures.

There's no bias audit yet. Redaction targets names, schools and cities, not
employers, graduation years or how someone writes. The model is told none of
that can affect a score, which is an instruction, not a guarantee.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The vote and the guard, as pseudocode:

```python
def triage(application, rubric):
    facts   = run_checks(application)             # deterministic; a person can verify each
    packet  = redact(application) + facts         # targets names, contacts, schools, cities
    samples = [score(packet, rubric, schema=SCHEMA) for _ in range(3)]

    for s in samples:                             # evidence or abstain
        s.drop_claims_not_quoted_verbatim(packet, application)

    craft   = median(s.craft for s in samples)
    ai      = median(s.ai_answer for s in samples)
    tier    = plurality([s.tier for s in samples], tie="maybe")
    signals = [sig for sig in reported_signals(samples) if votes(sig) >= 2]

    # Suspicion alone can never send someone to the bottom pile.
    if tier == "likely_pass" and not (facts.checked_problems
                                      or missed_must_haves(samples) >= 2
                                      or craft == 1):
        tier = "maybe"

    rank = (weighted(craft, ai, must_haves(samples))
            - penalties(facts.checked_problems)
            - small_penalty(model_suspicions(samples)))
    return tier, rank, flags(facts, signals), note(samples)


def write(row, fields):
    assert "Status" not in fields                 # every caller goes through here; a person moves Status
    db.update(row, fields)
```

## What's left for people

</Depth>

My first pass shrinks from 27 to 54 hours of reading to the 124 research
notes, at a minute or two each: two to four hours. The 660 maybes come after,
sorted so the strongest are on top. That's an estimate, and a bet: I'll only
know whether the 124 were the right 124 once I've made real decisions.

<Depth min={3}>

The first check wasn't encouraging. The calibration run compared the sort
with the research agents' earlier verdicts on those 91 applicants, not with
hiring outcomes, and it separated them only modestly better than a coin flip,
an AUC of 0.63. The earlier form didn't ask the AI question, the strongest
input now, so that's probably a floor. Every triage field is labeled
uncalibrated until there are fifty decisions on this wave to measure against.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The sort also can't see its own misses. Decisions only happen on people I read, so
nothing measures the bottom of the maybe pile unless I sample it on purpose.

</Depth>

The first pass also changes who gets to help. The research and the notes exist before
anyone talks to a candidate, so teammates can run the deeper interviews right
after my first call instead of waiting on me.

<Depth min={2}>

They don't need to be practiced interviewers. The research hands them
questions written for that candidate, and a short guide times the hour.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The guide takes about ten minutes to read. The pre-read goes in a fixed order:
the AI answer first, then the triage note, then the research. The hour-long
deep dive is planned to the minute. The candidate walks us through their work
for fifteen of those minutes, and later makes one small change live with their
usual tools. We're watching how they work, not only what ships. Everyone
scores alone before anyone discusses. From the guide: "Someone who made the
work can explain any piece of it. Someone who didn't, can't. You'll notice."
And next to the triage: "One flag means look closer. It never means reject."
Pay conversations stay with me.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

There's also a table of questions we never ask, with innocent-sounding
examples like "What year did you graduate?", and a section on what to do if
something seems off: ask them to open their own work and change something
small, never confront, and never treat an accent, a name, a time zone or a
quiet online presence as a sign of anything.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The work sample mirrors the roles: three to five hours across a week, one
piece in the applicant's own craft and one outside it. Structured interviews
and work samples have predicted job performance better than most methods for
decades, and a 2022 re-analysis that brought most of the numbers down still
put structured interviews on top.<Note id="3" /> AI just gets you to them
faster.

</Depth>
