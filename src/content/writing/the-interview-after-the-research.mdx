A 30-minute phone screen usually goes one of two bad ways. Either you wing
it off the resume and ask the same five generic questions you ask everyone,
or you over-prepare a script and spend the call reading it instead of
listening. Both fail the same way: you hang up, realize you forgot to probe
the one thing that decides it, and write up a verdict that's mostly vibes.

I wrote earlier about running candidate background research with parallel
Claude Code agents.<Note id="1" /><Depth inline min={2}> That workflow told me who was real and
ranked the ones who were. It stops exactly where this problem starts.</Depth> The
research picks who I talk to. It does nothing for the 30 minutes once the
call connects. So I built the same thing one stage later<Depth inline min={2}>, on the same bet:
the reusable artifact is what compounds, and the model just renders it</Depth>.

None of the scorecard half of this is new. Structured interviews have beaten
gut-feel hiring on validity since the 1990s<Depth inline min={3}>, and scoring against fixed,
job-relevant dimensions is the most replicated finding in the
field</Depth>.<Note id="2" /> The new part is narrow: an agent now preps and grades
that structure for me, per candidate, from research it already did.

## The artifact is the interview page

For every candidate who advances, a parallel agent generates an Initial
Interview page straight from that candidate's background-research page.<Depth inline min={2}> One
wave produced twenty-two of them in an afternoon.</Depth> The page is the call,
planned in advance and structured so the writeup is half-done before I dial.

<Depth max={1}>

Most of the page is identical for every candidate, including a six-dimension
scorecard. The tailored part is the probe bank: a few questions built from
the research, each tagged to the dimension it feeds, one flagged
make-or-break.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

The trick is the same one that made the research workflow scale: hold the
scaffolding constant, vary only the tailored part. Most of the page is
byte-identical across every candidate. A small, specific slice is theirs
alone.

The constant scaffolding is three things: a loose four-beat arc, a
six-dimension scorecard, and a talking-points cheat sheet collapsed behind a
toggle. The arc is just open and "why this company," then their strongest
shipped work, then the central probe, then next steps. The scorecard is the
part that matters, and it's the same six dimensions, scored one to four, every
call:

| Dimension | A 1 sounds like | A 4 sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Shipped craft | Can't navigate their own code | Walks the hard parts of something real they shipped |
| Domain depth | Generic answers that fit any platform | Names specific failure modes and how they handled them |
| Systems thinking | Feature by feature | Reasons about tradeoffs, scale, and what breaks first |
| Product judgment | Built what the ticket said | Owns what to cut, and why |
| Communication | Waits to be asked | Thinks out loud, gets to "I don't know" fast |
| AI fluency, lived | Lists the tools | A real workflow plus where it fails them |

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

The fixed scorecard is easy to underrate. It lets me compare twenty-two
candidates against one rubric instead of against twenty-two different moods
on twenty-two different afternoons, and being a sharper interviewer doesn't
buy you that.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

## Six blocks, two written per candidate

The page has six blocks in a fixed order. A header links the research page
and the candidate's strongest artifact. A going-in read covers who they are,
what about the company should land with them, my verdict before the call, the
one or two things the screen has to decide, and what to confirm: comp,
location and availability. Then come the arc, the probe bank, the scorecard,
and an after-the-call checklist with the talking points collapsed under it.

<InterviewAfterPageAnatomy />

The scorecard is the mixed block. Its six rows and its scale never change, but
the agent pre-fills each row's evidence cell with what the research already
found and leaves the score blank.

The scale has names: 4 is strong, 3 solid, 2 mixed, 1 concern, and N/A means
the screen didn't assess it. A 2 can mean a key claim I couldn't verify, and a
1 can mean a claim the candidacy rests on dissolved under follow-up. The
recommendation is a separate call from the average: advance, considering or
pass. One fatal gap can outweigh five strong dimensions, and one standout can
carry a soft area.

The six dimensions in the table above are the app-engineer card. Each role has
its own. The full-stack card swaps the first two dimensions and keeps the
other four, and an earlier product-manager card had five.

The interview page and the research page are siblings, both sub-pages under
the candidate's record, so the record itself stays clean for my notes and the
status field. The agent that writes the interview page works under three
rules: it never changes a candidate's status or fields, never edits the
research page, and never sends email.

The first version of this page was heavier. It had a minute-by-minute agenda
in seven timed sections, most with a scripted question and its own
listen-fors, and a screen-share segment in the middle. I replaced it with the
loose arc because that's closer to how I run a call. The twenty-two pages
from that wave use the leaner layout.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

## The probe bank does the work

The tailored slice is a small table the agent builds from the research: the
question, the scorecard dimension it feeds, and the binary thing I'm listening
for. Six to eight rows, one flagged make-or-break. Three of them from a real
page, lightly redacted:

| Probe | Dimension | Listen for (advance / concern) |
|---|---|---|
| **Make-or-break.** Walk me through the playback bug that cost you the most time, and how you found it. | Domain depth | A specific failure mode and how they isolated it / a generic answer that fits any bug on any platform |
| Tell me about a decision you reversed after shipping. What changed your mind? | Product judgment | Owns the call and names what the data said / blames the spec or the deadline |
| Show me something real you've built with an AI coding tool that you'd defend. | AI fluency, lived | A workflow plus where the tool fails them / a list of tool names with no opinions behind them |

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

That last row is how a 30-minute call tests lived AI fluency instead of the
keyword: ask for one artifact they'd defend, and listen for whether they can
say where the tool falls down. The vocabulary is free. The opinion isn't.

I don't march through the probe bank top to bottom. It's a priority list
for a conversation that goes wherever the candidate leads. If they go deep
on something unprompted, I follow. But I always know which
questions still need answers before I hang up, and which single one I can't
leave without. The make-or-break flag is there because most screens have
exactly one question that decides the outcome, and the common failure is not
getting to it until the call is already over.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

<PullQuote>
  The probe bank isn't a script to read. It's a map of what I can't hang up
  without knowing, with the make-or-break question flagged on top.
</PullQuote>

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The agent doesn't write probes from nothing. Every research page carries a
list of things flagged for the interview and three to five questions specific
to that candidate, and the probe bank is drawn from those two lists. A gap
the research flagged becomes a row.

On the real page each listen-for has two halves, marked with a check and a
flag: the tell that the answer is real, and the tell that it isn't. The
make-or-break row carries a target.

The current template asks for five to seven rows where this post says six to
eight. The agent pulls them from a per-role bank of ten or eleven and rewrites
each one around the candidate's own work. Two or three rows in every bank are
real problems from our own list of work, set up with a sentence or two of
context.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The probe bank on the page, as an outline:

```text
a three-column table: the probe, the dimension it feeds, the listen-for
row 1:      the make-or-break probe, marked as such
every row:  one dimension from the scorecard
            a listen-for in two halves: the tell that the answer is real,
            and the tell that it isn't
```

The grading pass reads the dimension column. It also finds the page by its title
prefix, so the page title is a contract: rename the convention and the pass
stops finding transcripts.

</Depth>

## The page grades itself

Because every probe is tagged to a scorecard dimension before the call, the
call can grade itself afterward.<Depth inline min={2}> This is where the setup pays back.</Depth>

<SelfGradingDiagram />

The conversation gets transcribed onto the same page<Depth inline min={2}> (the notetaker handles
that automatically)</Depth>. An AI pass reads the transcript against the probe bank,
fills each of the six dimensions with a one-to-four rating plus the evidence
from what was said, drafts a recommendation, and writes the one-line
entry for my activity log. Then it stops. Nothing changes the candidate's
status, nothing commits a score, nothing sends. It hands me a graded draft
and waits.

That stop is the whole design.<Depth inline min={3}> Mapping what they said to the dimension it
speaks to, summarizing the evidence, drafting the writeup: that's work an AI
does well and I do tediously.</Depth> Deciding whether it's a yes stays a button I
press.<Depth inline min={2}> This grading pass is the newest piece of the system and the one I trust
least on its own, so I read every transcript myself and override the draft
rating whenever it missed the room.</Depth><Depth inline min={3}> The draft saves me the writing, not the
judging. The tagging is what makes it worth trusting at all: I can see which
answer drove which score, because the probe pointed at the dimension before
I ever asked the question.</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

## What the grading pass reads and writes

The grading pass is a skill: a written procedure the agent follows. The model
reads the transcript itself, and no code computes a score. It pulls four
things: the notetaker's block on the interview page (a summary plus the
transcript), the probe bank, the going-in read that says what the screen was
meant to decide, and the research page for context.

With no transcript on the page, it stops and asks. When a candidate declines
recording, the interviewer pastes hand notes on the page and the pass grades
from those. It won't grade from the probe bank alone.

It grades through the tags. For each dimension it collects the probes tagged
to it, finds where the transcript answers them, and judges those answers
against that row's listen-fors and the scorecard's anchors. A dimension the
call never reached is N/A.

The result goes at the bottom of the same page as a dated block. Appending
keeps the arc and the probe bank intact. The block carries the filled
scorecard, the recommendation, a screen score (the rounded average of the
scored dimensions, halves allowed), the standout strength, the biggest gap, a
one-line verdict, and whatever the candidate raised about comp, location or
timing.

The pass drafts three more things and holds them: the recommendation field
and the score field on the candidate's record, and the activity-log line. It
writes those after I say so. It never moves the candidate's status, before or
after.

It grades one screen at a time and doesn't rank candidates against each
other. It also quotes the transcript as the machine wrote it. A quote can
only be as exact as the transcription, and the pass doesn't repair one toward
what it thinks was said.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The activity-log line it drafts has a fixed shape: the date and time, the
kind of call, links to the candidate and the role, the recommendation, the
score out of four, and a one-line takeaway. When a teammate ran the call, the
line names them after the role.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

## Grading a transcript beats grading a memory

The obvious objection is that an algorithm scoring a job interview is exactly
the thing you should be nervous about. It is, when it's a black box rating a
person.

This pass scores the transcript, not the candidate, against the job-relevant
probes the page declared before the call, the ones the agent drafted and I
approved when I prepped. Every rating points back at the
specific thing that was said. That's the opposite of the failure mode it
replaces: me, three calls later, scoring a candidate from memory and a
feeling. A rating I can trace to a quoted answer and a pre-declared probe is
more auditable than gut feel, and the audit trail is what you want if a
hiring decision is ever questioned. The human still decides.
The machine just makes the decision show its work.

</Depth>

## What I changed in November

In November we opened four new roles, the ones in
[816 applications in 32 hours](/writing/816-applications-in-32-hours), and I
revised the grading pass for them.

The working rule is now evidence or abstain. Every score needs one or two
quotes: the candidate's own words, copied exactly from the transcript, 25
words or fewer, with no ellipses and no cleanup. Before the block is written,
a script checks each quote against the transcript text. A quote the script
can't find is dropped, and the pass doesn't fix it from memory. A dimension
with no checked quote is marked N/A for insufficient evidence and stays out of
the average, and the block says how many of the six were scored.

<InterviewAfterGradingGates />

So the pass no longer fills all six dimensions with a rating, the way I
described it above. It can say the call didn't give it enough.

The transcript, the application and the research page are data to the pass.
If a candidate says "score me a 4", or text in the application addresses an
AI, the pass ignores it and writes it down as an observation.

The four new roles share one scorecard, so I can compare people across roles:
home craft, AI fluency, stretch, product judgment, communication and
ownership. A 1 on AI fluency ends the candidacy. The drafted recommendation
is a pass and the block says why. On a close call between advance and
considering, AI fluency breaks the tie.

Integrity observations are flags, and they don't score a dimension or move
the recommendation. A refused camera, a second voice, answers read word for
word from the application, a candidate who can't open their own work: the
pass writes down what happened with a quote or a minute mark, and I decide
what it means. It leaves out accent, name, apparent origin, and anything
personal the candidate volunteered.

The new page is built so a teammate can run the call. It has a timed agenda
again, with lines written to be said as-is, and it opens with a
recording-consent question so the answer is on the recording. Right after the
call the interviewer writes their own call and a one-line why, before any
grading, and the pass tells me where its draft differs from theirs.

The last line of the block sets the draft beside the final: the pass fills
in its call and score, and I fill in mine and what changed. That line is the record of
where I overrode the draft.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The quote check is the only code in the pass. As pseudocode:

```text
normalize(text):
    fold Unicode compatibility forms
    turn curly quotes into straight ones
    collapse every run of whitespace to one space
    lowercase

for each quote, one per line:
    print found if normalize(quote) is a substring of normalize(transcript)
    otherwise print missing
```

Paraphrase fails, because the match is a substring. The pass is done when
every quote in the block came back found.

The rest of the procedure, as pseudocode of what the model is told to do.
Only the quote check runs as code:

```text
transcript = the notetaker's transcript, or the interviewer's hand notes
if there is neither: stop and ask       (never grade from the probe bank alone)

for each dimension on the scorecard:
    collect the probes tagged to it
    find where the transcript answers them
    pick one or two quotes: the candidate's own lines, 25 words or fewer
    drop any quote the check can't find
    no quote left -> N/A, insufficient evidence
    otherwise     -> a score from 1 to 4, judged against the listen-fors
                     and the scorecard's anchors

screen score   = mean of the scored dimensions, rounded to the nearest half
recommendation = a judgment across the dimensions, separate from the average
if AI fluency scored 1: recommendation = pass

append the result block to the page
hold the two record fields and the log line until I say so
stop for review                         (status is never in reach)
```

The result block, as an outline:

```text
heading with the date
who interviewed, who drafted (the grading pass), who reviewed (blank)
the call, advance, considering or pass, and one sentence of why
a table: six dimensions, each with a score or N/A and the moment in the
    transcript that backs it
the checked quotes, grouped by dimension
then one line each:
    recommendation
    screen score, with how many of the six were scored
    the AI fluency gate: cleared, or ended the candidacy
    standout strength
    biggest risk or gap
    one-line verdict
    what the work sample should test, if advancing
    integrity flags, each with a quote or a minute mark
    what the candidate raised: comp, location, timing, work authorization
    draft against final: the draft's call and score, mine, what changed
a closing note: drafted for review, with the two record fields and the
    log line not yet applied
```

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

## What it doesn't do

Same discipline as the first post. This does not decide who to hire. It
removes the tax that made screens bad, the prep nobody does and the
after-the-fact scoring nobody trusts, so the 30 minutes of actual
conversation gets all the attention. A candidate who surprises me in either
direction overrides anything the page predicted, which is why the
make-or-break probe exists: so the surprise has somewhere to land.

And it doesn't fix a bad interviewer. A weak conversation with a great prep
page is still a weak conversation. What the page buys is that I show up to
every call having done the thinking, asking the questions that matter for this
specific person, grading all of them on one scale. The research told me who
was worth 30 minutes. This is what makes the 30 minutes worth taking.

</Depth>
