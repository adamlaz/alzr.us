I have shifted between founder mode and operator mode more than once over
the last two decades. Early in the arc I carried the same habits across
the line. They worked in founder mode and burned the operator chair
down. I had to learn that the two jobs use opposite muscles, not
adjacent ones, and the reflex that wins one is the reflex that loses the other.

Founder mode is deciding what gets built. Operator mode is deciding what
doesn't.

## The split

<Depth max={1}>

Founder mode is generative: the room is empty, so the bias is toward
action. Operator mode is selective: the room is full, so the bias is
toward saying no, because anything I say yes to gets built and a team
has to maintain it.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

Founder mode is generative. You stand at the front of an empty room and
the job is to populate it with the right things: the right product, the
right team, the right customers, the right narrative. The bias is toward
action, because nothing exists yet and the cost of inaction is much
higher than the cost of being slightly wrong about what to build. <Depth inline min={3}>You
move fast, decide a hundred small things a day, and revise as you learn.
Most days you can't tell whether you're winning, but you can usually tell
whether you're moving.</Depth>

Operator mode is selective. The room is full. The job is to make sure the
right things stay and the wrong things leave. The bias is toward saying
no, because anything you say yes to gets built, and the cost of building
the wrong thing now compounds across a team that has to maintain it. <Depth inline min={3}>You
move slower, make fewer decisions per day, and the decisions you make
matter more. The score is unclear; the calls you got wrong show up
three months later as a calendar of meetings that didn't need to happen.</Depth>

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

## Three things change when you shift

**Calendar shape.** In founder mode I spent most of my week building or
talking to clients, customers, and users. Two or three hours of meetings
a day, no more. In operator mode the calendar *is* the work. Five hours of meetings is a
light day, because the meetings are where the team negotiates which
things stay and which things leave. A founder hearing that thinks *that
sounds awful*, and is correct. It is awful as a founder. It is the job
as an operator.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

On the team I run now, in late 2026, the fixed part of that calendar is
small. Monday has a 30-minute kickoff, and Tuesday through Friday each
get a 15-minute standup: what you worked on, what's next, blockers. The
kickoff sets the week's outcomes and the standups report progress
against them.

<FounderOperatorWeek />

The kickoff runs in a fixed order. It opens on gaps, meaning any
committed outcome that is missing an owner, a date or a "done when"
line. Last week comes next, and each owner marks their commitments done,
partial or missed in the room. For this week, owners propose one to
three outcomes for each quarterly goal they carry. Blockers follow, then
two minutes on what ships Wednesday. A teammate runs the meeting. I run
only the last step: ship or slip, and approve the release.

The standup host rotates weekly through the team in alphabetical order.
The host keeps time and asks "anything parked?" before ending.

An earlier version of this failed. We had written standup updates, and
we retired them because the updates were exhaustive and nobody read
them. The replacement we agreed to try is a short recap after each
standup: blockers and who is clearing them, decisions, follow-ups with
owners, and parked items, with no per-person play-by-play. It runs as a
two-week trial with a keep-or-kill date.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The kickoff's first two tabs, as pseudocode of the board filters:

```python
STAGES  = ["Idea", "Candidate", "Committed", "In Progress", "Shipped", "Dropped"]
RESULTS = ["Proposed", "Done", "Partial", "Missed"]   # set by the owner, in the room

def gaps(outcomes):                  # the kickoff opens here
    return [o for o in outcomes
            if o.stage in ("Committed", "In Progress")
            and not (o.owner and o.target_date and o.done_when)]

def last_week(outcomes, cycle):      # each owner marks a result
    return [o for o in outcomes if o.cycle == cycle.previous]
```

A weekly commitment is an outcome row carrying the week and one of those
four results. Owners mark their own rows.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**Decision count.** Founders make dozens of decisions a day, most of them
small, most of them reversible. Operators make a handful a week, most of
them hard to reverse without a real cost to people on the team. When I
shifted, the number went down by an order of magnitude and the weight
went up by one. The first time a week felt quiet, I
realized I'd made three decisions in three meetings, and those three
were the whole week.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

The handful that matter get written down. Our product planning docs end
with a decision log, and each entry has five fields: status, date,
context, decision, consequences. The log is append-only. An accepted
entry never gets rewritten. A new entry supersedes it and the old one's
status flips.

Most decisions don't qualify. An entry gets written only when all three
of these hold: the decision is hard to reverse, it would surprise a
future reader who lacked the context, and it was a real trade-off with
alternatives someone could have picked. I took that test from Matt
Pocock's open-source
[skills repo](https://github.com/mattpocock/skills); the five fields
were already our format. Deliberate departures from the obvious path are
the entries worth the most, because they stop the next engineer from
"fixing" something that was intentional.

The log also showed me how loosely I talk. I told leadership "we can"
drop a vendor tool from next year's budget, and one of my agents filed
it as an accepted decision. It was a proposal. Now only "we will", "go",
"agree", "do it" or an explicit pick from options counts as decided. "We
can", "we could" and "I think we should" get filed as proposed, with the
question left open.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

Each entry is an architectural decision record, numbered from ADR-001
within its doc. The format, then the two rules as pseudocode:

```text
ADR-007        numbered per doc, in order
Status:        Proposed | Accepted | Superseded | Deprecated
Date:          YYYY-MM-DD
Context:       what forced the decision
Decision:      what was decided, in one or two sentences
Consequences:  what it enables, costs or rules out
```

```python
def worth_an_entry(d):               # all three, or skip it
    return (d.hard_to_reverse
            and d.surprising_without_context
            and d.real_trade_off)

def status_for(words):
    if words in ("we will", "go", "agree", "do it") or picks_an_option(words):
        return "Accepted"
    return "Proposed"                # "we can", "we could", "I think we should"
```

Unresolved questions stay in the doc's open-decisions list, and resolved
ones graduate into the log. Comments are where decisions happen. The
log is where they live.

</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

**Information shape.** In founder mode information came through whichever
channel was fastest: the client email, the bug report, the analytics
dashboard. In operator mode information comes through people. The team
is the filter. If the team is calibrated, the read is clear. If the team
is not calibrated, the read is distorted, and calibrating it is the
operator's job.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Calibrating starts with agreeing how to read the numbers before anyone
argues about what they mean. On a recent offer test my position was that
we wouldn't need statistical significance. We needed shared comfort that
the conclusions we drew from the reporting we had were reliable and
intuitive. That comes from using the same definitions every time, saying
which orders are left out and why, checking whether the pattern holds
across days, order sizes and sources, and knowing what a few big orders
swing. A read date written into a plan is a placeholder, so the plan
names the trigger instead.

The other channel is the one-on-one. With one direct report I run a
monthly development one-on-one that is explicitly not a status update,
because standup already owns the in-flight work. Their agenda goes
first, and my target is to talk 30% of the time. It opens with four
standing checks: what's on their mind, time off, a pulse on their
quarterly goals, and a friction check that asks what's in their way,
me included. When there's room, one rotating theme takes the rest:
growth, role and craft, level and pay, or feedback in both directions.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

The guardrails I keep open during that one-on-one:

- After a question, count to five before filling the silence.
- If it drifts tactical, say that standup owns that and go back to them.
- A level or pay question I can't answer in the room gets a date for a
  real answer. I never improvise pay.
- If I'm talking more than they are, stop, ask a question and wait.
- Never cancel. Only reschedule.
- Do my own action items before the next one.

</Depth>

## Four habits I had to break

Each one worked for me in founder mode and stopped working at the
operator boundary.

<Depth min={2}>

1. **Fix it yourself.** As a founder, the fastest path from "something is
   broken" to "something is shipped" was usually me. The first time I
   tried this from the operator chair, I patched three customer-facing
   bugs at midnight instead of paging the engineers who owned them. The
   bugs got fixed. The engineers noticed. The team noticed who noticed.
   Now I name the owner first and let the owner work.

2. **Fastest path to the answer.** Founders optimize for *getting* the
   answer. Operators optimize for *the team learning* the answer. The
   slower path through the team usually produces a worse answer once and
   a better team forever.

3. **The strong opinion in the room.** <Depth inline min={3}>As a founder, the strongest
   opinion in the room is usually yours, and that's fine: the company is
   small enough that the opinion is the strategy.</Depth> As an operator, the
   strongest opinion in the room is a self-silencing device. The whole
   meeting becomes a search for what the loudest person already decided.
   Now I ask the question first, listen second, and put my opinion in
   the room last, if at all.

4. **The all-hands tactical.** Founders use the all-hands to coordinate
   tactically: this week we're doing X, here's what to expect. Operators
   use the all-hands for the longer arc -- where we are, why this
   quarter matters, what changes if we win it. <Depth inline min={3}>The tactical work belongs
   in smaller forums where the trade-offs can be debated honestly. An
   all-hands that runs tactical is a sign the org's middle layer isn't
   doing its job.</Depth>

</Depth>

### The replacements as I run them now

Naming the owner has a hard edge. On a system a teammate owns, my part
ends at a pull request that is ready, a review guide, and a review
request. The owner makes any changes on the branch, merges, deploys, and
fixes it when it breaks. My note says to merge when they're confident.
It's their system: they get it live, and they are the one who repairs it
after it goes live. A rule I wrote down in 2023 says the same thing from
my side: stay out of the weeds so you can clear brush when needed,
because a leader executing on the critical path can't see the critical
path.

Opinion last has a written form. When I review someone else's quarterly
goals or planning doc, my comments go in as questions and suggestions:
"what do you think about", "worth considering". I'm direct only about
work I own. I still miss. In November 2026 I had a plan for a research
effort drafted in full, with tasks and a message to an outside
contractor, before the two people who own that work had weighed in. I
deleted the message and pitched the plan to the owners first, as an
idea, with the call left to them.

In the room, the guide I run retros from carries the same rule: if one
person starts driving the discussion, I ask the others by name what
they're hearing. The writing comes first, ten silent minutes of it
before anyone reads a note aloud. If nobody has posted something that is
slowing us down by minute five, I post one of mine, specific and a
little uncomfortable.

</Depth>

<Depth min={5}>

<FounderOperatorRetro />

The format is a sailboat: wind pushing us, anchors slowing us, rocks
ahead, and the island we're sailing toward. During the read-out nobody
defends or debates a note. Everyone gets three votes. Each discussed
item gets a four-minute timer, and the guide says not to solve it in the
meeting. Commitments are experiments, not policies, and each one needs
an owner and a date no more than two weeks out.

</Depth>

## Four habits I had to build

The new ones don't come naturally. They get practiced.

<Depth max={1}>

There are four. Ask the question the team hasn't thought to ask. Name
the trade-off in public. Defend the boring infrastructure work. Hold the
quality line when the team would ship.

</Depth>

<Depth min={2}>

1. **Ask the question that doesn't have an answer yet.** Operators get
   paid to ask the questions the team hasn't thought to ask. Most of
   mine arrive in the form *what would have to be true for this to break
   in six months?* <Depth inline min={3}>The question earns nothing in founder mode and earns
   its weight every quarter in operator mode.</Depth>

2. **Name the trade-off in public.** Founders make trade-offs in their
   head and ship the result. Operators name what got chosen, what got
   declined, and what the consequence of being wrong looks like, in front
   of the team. <Depth inline min={3}>The public-ness is what makes the decision reviewable --
   by the team, by the board, by future-me.</Depth>

3. **Defend the boring infrastructure work.** Nobody on the team lobbies
   for the migration that pays back in two quarters. The lobbying job is
   the operator's. <Depth inline min={3}>Founders ship features. Operators also ship the
   conditions that let the team keep shipping features.</Depth>

4. **Hold the quality line when the team would ship.** The hardest
   operator habit. Operators sometimes decide that something *should* be
   built better, even when the team is ready to ship it as-is. Holding
   that line looks like a brake. It is the spec.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

Holding the line is easier when the line is written before the work
starts. Every committed outcome on our board carries an owner, a date
and a "done when" line, and the kickoff opens on the outcomes missing
any of the three. Another rule from my 2023 notes covers the same
ground: define how you'll know it worked before you start, because
metrics chosen afterward rationalize what happened.

The best case I know for the boring infrastructure work is Will Larson's
2018 essay on [migrations](https://lethain.com/migrations/). He argues
they are usually the only route to meaningful progress on technical
debt, because the easy fixes get done by whoever can do them alone and
what remains needs many teams. A migration trades less output today for
more capacity later, which makes it controversial to schedule. The line
for operators comes near the end: when a team isn't working on a
migration, it is typically because their leadership hasn't prioritized
it. His essay on [saying no](https://lethain.com/saying-no/) offers a
scheduling tool for that argument, which is to set quotas for immediate
work and long-term work.

</Depth>

## Both modes live in the same week

Most engineering leaders I respect run both modes inside a single week,
not in separate phases of a career. A fifty-person org needs zero-to-one
initiatives *and* selective pruning, often from the same person,
sometimes the same day. <Depth inline min={3}>I treat them as serial here because the
*shift* is what's hard to talk about. Inside any given role they live
next to each other on the calendar.</Depth> The hard part is rarely choosing a
mode. It's noticing which mode the room needs this hour, and finding
the reflex for it on demand.

</Depth>

## On "Founder Mode"

Paul Graham's "Founder Mode" essay landed in 2024 and got broader
treatment than it asked for.<Note id="1" /> The essay names a real thing: founders
run companies differently than career managers, and at some sizes the
founder's way produces strictly better results. <Depth inline min={2}>The argument is precise
on its narrow claim.</Depth>

My argument here is in tension with that one. <Depth inline min={2}>Paul Graham's
essay reads as a permission slip to stay in founder mode longer than the
career-manager class will tell you to. Mine is the opposite
permission slip. The same instincts that make a great zero-to-one CEO
damage a 200-person org.</Depth> Founder mode is a mode you can stay in
too long. Operator mode is a mode some founders never learn to enter,
and the company eats itself when they don't.

Both arguments are true. <Depth inline min={3}>Founder mode is an undervalued operating style
at scale, and operator mode is an underwritten one for the same person.</Depth>
The useful question is whether you can move between them when the
company needs you to.<Depth inline min={3}> Andy Grove's *High
Output Management* and Will Larson's *An Elegant Puzzle* are both
operator-mode books written without apology for the operator side of the
work.</Depth><Note id="2" />

<Depth min={4}>

### What the essay says on a close read

The essay came out of a talk Brian Chesky gave at a Y Combinator event.
In Graham's telling, advisers told Chesky as Airbnb grew to hire good
people and give them room to do their jobs. He followed the advice, the
results were bad, and he worked out his own way, partly by studying how
Steve Jobs ran Apple. Graham calls the advised style manager mode and
compares it to modular design: treat each subtree of the org chart as a
black box, tell direct reports what to do, and stay out of how.

Founder mode he leaves undefined. He writes that he knows of no books
about it. He predicts skip-level meetings will become normal, and gives
one example: the annual retreat Jobs ran for the 100 people he
considered most important at Apple, who were not the 100 highest on the
org chart. Then he says nobody knows yet whether that retreat is a good
idea.

The broader treatment lost the concession. "There's going to have to be
some amount of delegation," he writes, since a founder can't run 2,000
people the way they ran 20. Where the borders of autonomy sit will vary
by company, and over time inside one company as managers earn trust. His
third footnote predicts the misuse: founders who can't delegate will use
founder mode as the excuse, and managers who aren't founders will try to
act like founders. The essay names no size at which the founder's way
wins. Twenty and 2,000 are its only numbers.

So the tension between us is narrower than I framed it. His two modes
sort by how far down the org chart the CEO reaches. Mine sort by whether
the job is adding or pruning. A founder can reach three levels down and
still be the person deciding what doesn't get built.

### Grove and Horowitz on the same question

For Grove I'm leaning on Ben Horowitz's
[2015 essay](https://a16z.com/2015/11/13/high-output-management/) on the
book. He gives Grove's definition of a manager's output as the output of
their organization plus the output of the neighboring organizations
under their influence, and draws the consequence: what a manager knows
is worth nothing until it transfers to the team. That is the second
habit I had to break, written as an equation. He also summarizes Grove's
idea of task-relevant maturity. Whether to manage hands-on or hands-off
depends on how mature the person is in that specific task. Graham's
borders of autonomy that move as managers earn trust are the same dial.

Horowitz's own
[2011 post](https://a16z.com/peacetime-ceo-wartime-ceo/), which shares
its name with the chapter in my note, splits by the company's situation.
Peacetime is a large lead in a growing market. Wartime is an imminent
existential threat. He asks whether one CEO can do both and answers yes,
but it's hard, because it takes knowing the rules of management and when
to break them. He also puts Grove on the wartime side, and says most
management books describe peacetime methods, Grove's excepted. My shelf
of operator-mode books is less tidy than the sentence above makes it
sound.

</Depth>

## What I'm still bad at

Holding the quality line, mostly. Letting the team ship while disagreeing
with them out loud about the spec. The founder reflex says *ship it,
revise after.* The operator reflex says *the spec is the spec and we
agreed.* <Depth inline min={2}>Both reflexes are useful. Knowing which one to run on a Tuesday
afternoon is a skill I have not finished learning.</Depth>

<Depth min={3}>

Also: operator mode as the only person in the room running it. Earlier
in my career I worked alongside people whose dispositions covered the
modes I wasn't running. *Operator mode in the abstract* and *operator
mode when the team is calibrating to whether you'll actually sit in the
chair* are different shapes of the same skill, and the second one only
shows up when nobody else is doing it for you.

</Depth>

<Depth min={4}>

One count of the founder reflex surviving: in November 2026 a cleanup of
my own task board took my in-progress list from 51 rows to 13, one row
for each thing I was driving that week. A list of 51 says I was still
saying yes by default.

</Depth>

When I shift again I want to read this and find at least one claim I now
think is wrong. That's the test of whether the work was actual learning
or just a clean story.
